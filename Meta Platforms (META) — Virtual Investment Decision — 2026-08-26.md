## Why I am buying Meta now

I am establishing a USD 10,000 virtual position in Meta because I think the market is still treating several parts of the company too separately.

The conventional view is easy to understand. Meta owns Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp and Messenger. It has a huge advertising business. It is spending an extraordinary amount of money on AI infrastructure. It is also taking on large legal, regulatory and capital-allocation risks. If the AI buildout earns poor returns, the stock can disappoint even if AI itself becomes important.

I agree with all of that.

The reason I am investing anyway is that I think Meta has a stronger economic system around AI than a simple segment-by-segment description suggests.

The company already owns distribution to billions of people, a social graph, messaging networks, advertiser relationships, conversion data, recommendation systems, a growing AI assistant, a large internal AI research effort and an increasingly serious hardware layer through smart glasses. It is now adding an infrastructure base large enough to support training, inference, experimentation and future agentic products at enormous scale.

The important question is not whether every one of these businesses becomes dominant.

The question is whether their combination allows Meta to turn additional intelligence and compute into economic value faster than the market currently expects.

That is the bet.

I am not buying Meta because I think it will necessarily build the best model in the world. I am not assuming that Meta AI becomes the dominant personal assistant. I am not assuming that smart glasses replace the smartphone. I am not assuming Reality Labs suddenly becomes a major profit center. I am not assuming that every dollar of AI capex earns an extraordinary return.

The position works if a much smaller set of things goes right: the core advertising and recommendation engine remains strong, AI continues to improve monetization, the infrastructure build eventually produces enough incremental cash flow to justify the capital committed, and Meta retains enough access to the user to keep its distribution and feedback advantages economically valuable.

That is a much lower hurdle than the most aggressive AI bull case.

---

## The existing business matters more than the futuristic story

Meta is not a pre-revenue AI company waiting for a future product to justify its valuation.

In Q2 2026, revenue reached **USD 60.8 billion**, up **28% year over year**. The company still generates enormous operating cash flow even while the current infrastructure build is crushing near-term free cash flow. That distinction matters.

I do not need to value Meta primarily on a hypothetical future agent business.

The existing Family of Apps business already provides a large economic engine capable of funding experimentation and infrastructure. AI is therefore being deployed into a business that already has users, advertisers, conversion events, ranking systems, distribution and measurable outcomes.

That gives Meta something many AI companies do not have: a direct way to test whether additional intelligence creates money.

A better recommendation system can be deployed to real users. A better advertising model can be tested against clicks and conversions. A new creative model can be measured against advertiser outcomes. A business agent can be evaluated against actual conversations and transactions.

This creates a closed loop:

**compute → model improvement → deployment → real user response → measured economic outcome → new data → better model**

That loop is one of the main reasons I am willing to own the company while capex is so high.

The infrastructure is not being built next to a business with no distribution. It is being attached to one of the largest real-world experimentation surfaces in the technology industry.

---

## Reality feedback may be more valuable as intelligence becomes cheaper

One of my broader investment assumptions is that AI does not eliminate scarcity. It moves scarcity.

As the cost of generating software, text, images, analysis, hypotheses and possible actions falls, the ability to test those outputs against reality becomes relatively more important.

It is easy to generate ten thousand ad variants if model inference becomes cheap.

It is harder to know which variation actually changes human behavior.

It is easy to generate a thousand recommendations.

It is harder to know which recommendation causes a user to remain engaged, buy something, message someone or complete a useful action.

Meta has an unusually large feedback surface for exactly this reason.

The company does not just generate predictions. It can deploy them into live consumer systems and observe what happens.

If intelligence becomes abundant, that reality-feedback loop can become a residual scarce asset.

This is not a claim that the loop has infinite value. It is a claim that the loop can make a unit of compute more economically useful at Meta than it would be inside a company with weaker distribution and slower feedback.

The variable I care about most is therefore not raw GPU count.

It is what I call **Deployment Learning Rate**: how quickly Meta can turn an additional unit of compute into a newly discovered profitable use case.

If Meta can repeatedly use its distribution to discover where AI produces money, the company can create future demand for the infrastructure it is building today.

That is a much more interesting mechanism than simply forecasting how many chatbot prompts users will send.

---

## Compute demand can be created by the search for use cases

A standard demand model assumes that future AI demand exists somewhere outside the company and management has to estimate it.

I think that is incomplete.

Compute can itself help create future demand.

Meta can use compute for research, simulation, evaluation, experimentation, recommendation improvements, ad models, agents and product discovery. A failed experiment can still reduce uncertainty and reveal where not to invest. A successful experiment can create a new production workload that then consumes even more compute.

The loop can therefore become:

**compute today → experimentation → discovery → new profitable workload → higher compute demand tomorrow**

This is one reason visible present-day demand may understate the rational amount of future capacity.

The strongest version of the bull case is not that management already knows every workload that will consume the infrastructure.

It is that Meta has built a system capable of discovering those workloads faster than competitors.

That is also why I do not treat temporary spare capacity as automatic proof of overbuilding. Some excess capacity can function as an option on future demand discovery, especially when power, interconnections and data-center capacity take much longer to secure than a new AI use case takes to discover.

But this argument has a limit. Optionality only has value if the company eventually finds high-return uses for the capacity. Infrastructure cannot be justified forever by the possibility that something useful will appear later.

That is where the bear case begins.

---

## The real bear case is marginal ROIC, not lack of AI usage

The strongest argument against Meta is not that AI will fail or that the data centers will sit empty.

They can be full and the investment can still be bad.

The real question is the return on the marginal dollar of capital.

Meta can have excellent returns on the first wave of AI spending because recommendation systems and ad matching have obvious high-value use cases. That does not mean the last tens of billions of dollars invested in a much larger infrastructure base will earn the same return.

There may be diminishing returns.

The first improvement in a recommendation model can create a large monetization uplift. The next improvement can create a smaller uplift. Eventually the company may need increasingly expensive compute to produce increasingly small economic gains.

At the same time, depreciation, leases, power commitments, networking costs and hardware obsolescence remain real.

This creates the central test of the position:

**Does Meta's Deployment Learning Rate stay high enough to offset declining marginal returns in already-optimized products?**

If the answer is yes, the current buildout can create a long runway of new profitable workloads.

If the answer is no, Meta can become a victim of the same capital-cycle logic that affects other infrastructure-heavy industries: early scarcity produces aggressive investment, aggressive investment produces abundant supply, and returns on the final unit of capital fall sharply.

This risk is large enough that I am not sizing Meta above my highest-upside positions.

---

## The infrastructure commitment is already real

The current capex program is not a vague future promise.

Meta expects **2026 capital expenditures, including principal payments on finance leases, of USD 130–145 billion**.

The Q2 2026 10-Q also disclosed **USD 349.31 billion of non-cancelable contractual commitments** as of June 30, 2026, with approximately **USD 53.52 billion due in 2026** and **USD 81.65 billion due in 2027**. These commitments relate mainly to cloud capacity, servers, network infrastructure, data centers and Reality Labs hardware.

Meta also had **USD 10.80 billion of restricted cash equivalents** tied to escrow requirements under multi-year infrastructure purchase agreements.

In addition, the company disclosed large lease obligations for data centers and related infrastructure that had not yet commenced, as well as additional data-center leases signed in July.

I do not add all of these figures together mechanically because different categories can have different accounting treatment and potentially overlapping economic meaning. The important point is simpler: a large part of the future infrastructure burden is already contractual rather than purely discretionary.

That changes the risk profile.

Management still has flexibility over some future server and accelerator purchases, especially where hardware becomes obsolete quickly. It has much less flexibility over infrastructure that requires long lead times: land, buildings, power, interconnections and long-duration contracts.

The strategy can be rational because these are exactly the assets that may become scarce first.

But if demand disappoints, that same long-duration commitment becomes a fixed-cost problem.

---

## Speed-to-power is a strategic asset

One reason I am more sympathetic to Meta's aggressive buildout than a simple capex chart would suggest is the mismatch between AI product cycles and physical infrastructure cycles.

A new AI workload can become economically attractive very quickly.

A gigawatt-scale data-center site cannot appear quickly.

Power availability depends on geography, grid connections, generation, transformers, permitting, land and construction schedules. The relevant resource is not abstract electricity. It is reliable, connected power in the right place at the right time.

This creates a timing asymmetry.

Meta can discover a new profitable use for compute in months and then discover that the physical capacity required to scale it takes years to deliver.

Securing long-lead infrastructure before every use case is visible can therefore be rational.

The investment case becomes much stronger if Meta is correctly buying future scarcity before the market fully prices it.

It becomes much weaker if the company is simply locking itself into a giant fixed-cost structure at the top of an industry investment cycle.

I do not know which outcome is certain. The position exists because I think the first outcome has enough probability and enough economic upside to justify the risk at the current valuation.

---

## Meta does not need the best model

A large part of the market discussion still treats AI competition as though the company with the best benchmark score necessarily captures the most economic value.

I do not think that is how the final system will work.

Model capability is one input.

Distribution, identity, permissions, context, devices, feedback, orchestration and monetization matter too.

Meta can create large economic value with a model that is merely good enough if that model is embedded inside products used by billions of people and connected to a highly effective advertising and messaging system.

The company also has the option to use different models for different tasks over time. It does not need to make every future workload dependent on a single internal frontier model.

If model capability becomes more commoditized, Meta's distribution and feedback assets may become relatively more valuable.

If frontier capability remains scarce, owning large amounts of compute and internal research capability remains valuable.

This gives the company exposure to more than one plausible AI market structure.

---

## Interconnected AI makes Meta more interesting, but I am not paying for it as the base case

This investment also connects to my broader Interconnected AI thesis.

I think computing is gradually moving away from isolated applications and devices toward persistent AI systems that maintain state across software, sensors, services and physical devices.

Meta has several assets that become more valuable in that world: messaging, social context, identity, continuous consumer interaction and smart glasses.

The glasses are especially interesting because Meta does not control the dominant smartphone operating systems.

A phone-level personal AI controlled by Apple or Google can become a serious strategic problem for Meta. Apple controls iOS. Google controls Android. They control permissions, defaults, system integration and access to many sensors and background services.

If the next major AI layer lives primarily at the operating-system level, Meta risks remaining an application inside someone else's orchestration system.

Glasses can partially bypass that problem by giving Meta its own persistent sensor and interface layer close to the user.

But I deliberately treat this as optionality rather than a requirement.

I do not need glasses to replace phones for the investment to work. I do not need Meta to dominate personal AI. I do not need a fully unified cross-device state architecture to emerge on Meta's terms.

If those things happen, they provide upside.

The USD 10,000 position is justified primarily by the existing business, the AI monetization loop and the possibility that the current infrastructure build earns strong returns.

---

## The OS handicap is one reason the position is not larger

Alphabet and Apple possess something Meta does not: control over major consumer operating systems.

That matters more in an agentic world than it did in a simple app world.

An AI agent that can see calendars, notifications, files, location, sensors, contacts, payments and device state has enormous orchestration power. The operating system naturally sits close to those permissions.

Meta can own the social graph and still lose control of the highest-level personal agent.

This creates a form of platform hold-up risk.

Apple or Google can change APIs, permissions, defaults or product integration in ways that reduce Meta's ability to capture the full value of its user context.

Meta's distribution is still enormous, so I do not think this makes the company structurally weak. But it prevents me from treating the consumer AI opportunity as uncontested.

It is one of the reasons Meta receives USD 10,000 rather than the larger allocation I reserve for a thesis with both high expected upside and fewer structural dependencies.

---

## The legal risk is not a footnote

The legal and regulatory situation has to be part of the investment decision, not something added after the valuation work.

Meta is currently facing a major federal trial in Oakland brought by a bipartisan coalition of **29 U.S. states**. The states allege that Facebook and Instagram were designed in ways that harmed young users and that Meta misled the public about safety and improperly collected data from children under 13.

The plaintiffs have discussed extremely large penalty calculations. Reuters has reported that the states may seek nearly **USD 200 billion** in penalties in the current trial, while Meta has previously said certain state calculations could reach as high as **USD 1.4 trillion** under aggressive legal theories.

I do not treat either number as an expected loss.

They are claim magnitudes, not probability-weighted valuation deductions.

The more important risk is that courts or regulators impose product changes.

A large one-time fine would hurt, but Meta can financially survive a great deal. A structural restriction on recommendation systems, engagement design, data use, age verification or feed mechanics can damage the exact feedback loops that make the company valuable.

This is therefore not merely a legal cash-flow risk.

It is a product-economics risk.

Meta already recognized **USD 2.4 billion of charges related to legal proceedings in Q2 2026**, which caused the company to raise the lower end of its full-year expense outlook.

The risk is real enough to affect sizing, but not strong enough at this point to invalidate the investment.

## Valuation: I do not need the full bull case

The most important reason I am willing to buy Meta despite these risks is that the stock does not appear to require every part of the qualitative thesis to become true.

At roughly the current share price, my previous reverse-valuation work suggested that Meta needs a normalized long-run free-cash-flow margin in roughly the low-20s under central discount-rate and terminal-growth assumptions, with the working estimate around **22%**.

That is not a trivial hurdle.

The current infrastructure build can permanently reduce free-cash-flow conversion if depreciation and reinvestment remain structurally high. A company that historically produced extraordinary cash flow can still disappoint if AI makes the business permanently more capital intensive.

But the hurdle also does not require a science-fiction outcome.

Meta does not need to dominate AGI, replace Apple, win every device category or turn Reality Labs into the next iPhone business.

The core advertising business can do much of the work if revenue growth remains healthy, operating economics remain strong and the capex cycle eventually normalizes enough for cash flow to recover.

That is what makes the current risk/reward attractive to me.

The upside can come from multiple additional sources, while the base-case requirement is still anchored in a business that already produces enormous revenue and operating cash flow.

## Why the position is USD 10,000 and not USD 15,000

Position size should express the combination of expected return, confidence and downside structure.

I currently think Xiaomi has greater upside if my Interconnected AI thesis is correct because the market can eventually reclassify the economic value of Xiaomi's physical device graph, ecosystem and orchestration potential. That creates more room for a large change in how the company is valued.

Meta is different.

It is already one of the largest and most closely followed companies in the world. The market already understands that it has powerful distribution, a huge advertising business and major AI ambitions. My edge, if one exists, has to come from being more correct about the economic interaction between compute, experimentation, reality feedback, deployment learning and future consumer interfaces than the price currently implies.

I think that edge can be real, but it is narrower than owning a company whose entire strategic category may be misclassified.

At the same time, Meta is more robust than many smaller or more speculative AI exposures because the core business is already extremely profitable.

That combination makes USD 10,000 the right current size for me.

It is large enough to indicate high conviction.

It is not large enough to pretend that the capital cycle, legal risk, operating-system dependence and uncertain marginal AI returns have already been solved.

I would consider increasing the position later if the evidence improves.

## What would make me add

I would become more comfortable increasing the position if Meta begins to show a clear free-cash-flow inflection after the current buildout, while revenue and operating income continue to grow strongly.

I would also want more evidence that incremental AI investment continues to produce measurable economic gains rather than merely higher usage or more model capability.

The strongest positive signals would be continued monetization improvements in ads and recommendations, economically meaningful business-agent adoption, rising usage intensity rather than just headline user counts, evidence that Meta can turn additional capacity into new profitable workloads, greater independence from Apple and Google through devices or other interfaces, and legal outcomes that avoid major structural restrictions on the core recommendation and engagement systems.

If those developments occur while valuation remains reasonable, the case for moving above USD 10,000 becomes much stronger.

---

## What would make me reduce or exit

The thesis weakens materially if Meta's capex remains structurally enormous without a corresponding recovery in free cash flow.

I would also become concerned if management continues to expand infrastructure commitments while evidence of marginal monetization becomes weaker.

A full data center is not enough. High AI usage is not enough. Better benchmark scores are not enough.

The investment needs economic returns.

Other serious negative signals would include Apple or Google establishing a personal-agent layer that materially reduces Meta's access to context or user action, legal remedies that directly weaken recommendation and advertising economics, persistent deterioration in the Family of Apps core, or evidence that the company needs permanently rising capital intensity merely to defend its existing position.

A falling share price by itself is not a kill criterion.

A broken causal chain is.

## Probability discipline

I am deliberately not attaching an exact probability such as 75%, 82% or 63% to Meta outperforming the S&P 500.

Earlier work on this investment became too precise relative to the quality of the underlying probability model.

The variables that matter most — marginal AI ROIC, long-run capex intensity, operating-system capture, legal remedies, terminal valuation and the speed of new workload discovery — do not yet have distributions that I can defend with that level of precision.

My conclusion is therefore simpler:

I think the expected return is above the S&P 500 from the current price, and I think the probability of positive alpha is high enough to justify a high-conviction position.

I do not yet think the evidence justifies pretending that I know the exact percentage chance.

That distinction matters.

The purpose of the portfolio record is not to manufacture certainty. It is to preserve what I believed, why I believed it, what evidence would change my mind and how much capital I was willing to put behind the belief at the time.

---

## Final investment thesis

The Meta position is ultimately a bet on one question:

**Can Meta turn the next massive unit of AI capital into sustainable economic value faster than the marginal return on that capital declines?**

The bull case is that Meta has one of the best systems in the world for doing exactly that.

It owns enormous distribution. It can deploy models into live consumer products. It can measure behavior and conversions. It can learn from those results. It can use the learning to improve models and products. It can then reinvest the resulting cash flow into more compute and more experimentation.

The loop is:

**capital → compute → experimentation → reality feedback → deployment learning → profitable workloads → revenue and cash flow → more capital**

If this loop remains strong, the current infrastructure build can look excessive before it looks prescient.

The bear case is that the loop weakens at the margin.

Meta may discover that the easiest high-return AI improvements were captured early. The next wave of compute can produce smaller monetization gains. Apple and Google can retain control over the operating-system layer. Regulation can weaken the recommendation and engagement systems. Long-duration infrastructure commitments can become a fixed-cost burden. Free cash flow can remain structurally below what the current valuation requires.

Both worlds are plausible.

At approximately USD 570 per share, I think the first world is underpriced enough relative to the second to justify owning the stock.

I am therefore recording a **USD 10,000 virtual investment in Meta Platforms**.

This is a high-conviction position, but not my highest-upside position.

The next stage is not to become more attached to the thesis. It is to watch whether the company proves the thing that actually matters: that its extraordinary AI spending produces extraordinary incremental economic returns.
