## Why I am selling the entire position

I am closing my Tokyo Ohka position in full, including the capital allocated weeks ago and the additional $10,000 allocated immediately before this rally. I am not trimming the new allocation while keeping the old shares. The decision applies to all exposure.

My entry thesis was that the market had marked down a strategically important semiconductor-materials supplier faster than the available operating evidence justified. Tokyo Ohka's photoresists, high-purity chemicals, qualification relationships and process-level switching costs made the underlying business interesting. The rapid price decline gave me the possibility of purchasing those economics at a more attractive valuation.

That was the opportunity I wanted to capture. It was not a claim that I could forecast the upcoming quarterly report.

The stock has now rebounded sharply ahead of the November 10 earnings release. The roughly 7% move does not establish that the entire valuation gap has closed. But it *does* change the trade. At the new price, part of the near-term dislocation I was trying to exploit may already have been corrected. My prospective return from continuing to own the shares is lower than it was at the distressed entry price, while the uncertainty associated with the pending results remains.

The question at this point is not whether my original entry was intelligent. The question is whether I would allocate the entire approximately $17,000 again, at today's price, immediately ahead of an earnings announcement whose surprise relative to expectations I cannot reliably predict.

I cannot justify doing that with the same confidence as the original purchase.

## Holding is a new allocation decision

I do not consider holding a stock to be passive. Every day I retain the exposure, I am choosing it over cash and every alternative investment available to me.

That distinction matters especially after a rapid gain. A successful entry can turn into an unattractive hold if the price adjusts before the evidence underlying the original thesis has materially changed. Selling is not an admission that the business is weak. It is an acknowledgment that *price, expected return, and risk* have changed.

I do not want to keep a position simply because the trade has worked so far, because I am attached to the research, or because a still-higher price might validate my judgment further. Those are not reasons to risk capital through a binary information event.

## Why I am not holding through earnings

Tokyo Ohka's November 10 report may confirm substantial demand for advanced materials and favorable semiconductor manufacturing trends. I still think the company occupies an interesting position in the semiconductor supply chain. Nothing in this exit decision is a new claim that its moat has disappeared.

But a strong business and a strong earnings reaction are different things. The stock can fall on good results if expectations are higher; it can rally on weak results if investors feared worse. Determining which outcome is already reflected in the price requires better evidence than a broad belief that the business is sound.

I had a reason to buy the depressed valuation. I do not have an equally strong, independently grounded reason to carry the full position through the earnings event after the sharp rebound.

My preference is to realize the opportunity that has presented itself rather than transform it into a directional earnings bet.

There is a cost to this conservatism: Tokyo Ohka may continue higher immediately, and I could miss a substantial rally. I accept that opportunity cost. Avoiding a poorly justified event risk is more important to this decision than maximizing the payoff in every favorable scenario.
