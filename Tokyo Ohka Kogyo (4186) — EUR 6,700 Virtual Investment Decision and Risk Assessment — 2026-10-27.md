# Tokyo Ohka Kogyo (4186) — Why I Am Allocating €6,700

**Decision date:** October 27, 2026  
**Company:** Tokyo Ohka Kogyo Co., Ltd. (TSE: 4186)  
**Decision:** **BUY — €6,700 allocation recorded in my virtual/paper investment portfolio**  
**Investment risk:** **4/5 — high**  
**Reference price:** **¥7,750**, Tokyo closing price on October 27, 2026  
**Horizon:** At least through 2030, subject to changes in the underlying thesis  
**Execution detail:** The €6,700 amount is my confirmed *virtual allocation decision*. The reference closing price is not a verified trade fill; the exact simulated share quantity, EUR/JPY conversion and execution timestamp have not been reconciled. This is not a claim of a real-money brokerage purchase.

## Why I am buying

I have spent a lot of time looking for businesses that could become more valuable as intelligence becomes cheaper.

My starting point is that AI makes certain kinds of work easier: writing software, designing systems, analyzing information and solving technical problems. But making the intellectual part of a task cheaper does not make its physical execution free. Somebody still has to build the chips, manufacture the equipment, supply the materials and operate the infrastructure.

I expect that distinction to matter more over time.

Tokyo Ohka Kogyo is interesting to me because it sells highly specialized materials used in semiconductor manufacturing. It makes photoresists, high-purity chemicals and materials used in advanced packaging and wafer handling. The company is not trying to predict which AI application will win. Its business depends on whether the next generation of semiconductors continues to require the chemistry and precision manufacturing it knows how to provide.

That is an attractive place to look for an investment. It is not enough, by itself, to justify buying the stock.

The market knows that AI demand is increasing and that modern chips are becoming harder to manufacture. My actual bet is more specific: Tokyo Ohka may be able to apply its existing technical capabilities to a growing number of valuable semiconductor processes, with the resulting cash flow improving more than investors currently expect.

I am putting €6,700 into the virtual portfolio rather than a larger amount because I think that opportunity is credible, but the valuation still leaves meaningful room for disappointment.

## What makes this business difficult to replace

Photoresists are an unusually sensitive part of chipmaking. They help transfer patterns onto wafers during lithography. At advanced manufacturing nodes, a small difference in material performance can affect defects, yield and the economics of an entire production line.

A customer that has already qualified a material has a reason not to change it casually. Requalifying a replacement costs time, money and engineering resources, and a failed transition can be much more expensive than any savings on the material itself.

The hard-to-copy advantage is therefore not simply a formula. It is the combination of chemical expertise, purity, consistent production, process knowledge and years of qualification alongside major semiconductor manufacturers.

Tokyo Ohka has built a substantial position in this industry. Its research materials cite roughly **24.7% of the global photoresist market**. That figure refers to the broad market, not a 24.7% share of EUV photoresists specifically.

There is a limitation I take seriously. Being an established supplier today does not guarantee winning the next generation of processes. JSR, Shin-Etsu, Fujifilm and other established players are capable competitors. New approaches, including metal-oxide and dry-resist technologies, could change where value accrues. A customer may be reluctant to replace a qualified product on an existing line while still choosing another supplier for a new process.

I rate Tokyo Ohka's current competitive position highly. I am less certain about how much of that position will survive unchanged through the next decade. That distinction matters for the risk rating.

## The demand is already visible

I do not need to assume that AI will eventually create some completely new market for Tokyo Ohka. The company has already reported growth in products associated with advanced semiconductor manufacturing.

In 2025, EUV photoresist sales grew approximately **50%** and wafer-handling-system materials approximately **40%**, according to its reporting. The company also supplies materials used in high-bandwidth-memory production.

These growth rates should be read carefully. They refer to product categories, not the entire company. A fast-growing product can still be a relatively small part of revenue, and its growth cannot be applied indiscriminately to the whole business.

Even so, the direction makes economic sense. Modern logic chips, HBM, chiplets and more complex packaging put demanding requirements on patterning, bonding, cleaning and material reliability.

I see a reasonable possibility that the value of specialized materials per advanced semiconductor process increases over time. The amount of computing deployed also matters. Better algorithms and more efficient chips may reduce resources needed per individual task, while cheaper computation may increase total usage. The net effect on wafer demand is uncertain. I am not treating every AI-related announcement as incremental semiconductor sales.

The upside depends on actual production volumes, the processes where Tokyo Ohka wins qualification, and the economics of the products it supplies.

## Why the existing technical base could become more valuable

This is the most important part of the investment for me.

I prefer growth opportunities where a company can reuse something it already knows how to do. Tokyo Ohka has decades of experience in specialized chemistry and semiconductor process control. Some of that knowledge is useful across several types of photoresists, high-purity chemicals, wafer-handling materials and packaging applications.

If the company can sell additional qualified materials into more processes using overlapping research, production expertise and customer relationships, revenue may rise faster than the cost of recreating those capabilities.

That would be attractive operating leverage.

The stronger version of the thesis is an improvement in **incremental return on invested capital**: additional after-tax operating profit growing faster than the capital required to produce it.

I am not assuming this has already happened. Semiconductor materials are not software. Facilities, purification, quality control, working capital and new research programmes all require capital. New factories also require orders and high utilization before they become economically productive.

The distinction is straightforward. More factories and higher revenue are not the goal. More cash generated for each additional unit of capital is the goal.

## Japan, the United States and industrial coordination

I also think Japan's place in the semiconductor supply chain is relevant.

The United States has important positions in chip design, cloud platforms, AI infrastructure and semiconductor research. Japan has unusually deep expertise in materials, industrial chemistry and precision manufacturing. South Korea, Taiwan and European partners supply other critical parts of the system.

Closer cooperation could make it easier for suppliers such as Tokyo Ohka to participate in new projects, qualify materials earlier and serve manufacturing plants across more regions.

The political and industrial coordination is real. Tokyo Ohka's relationships and technical cooperation in the United States, alongside its existing Asian manufacturing network, give it a plausible way to benefit.

But there is a big difference between a new fab being announced and extra cash reaching a materials supplier.

A factory needs to begin actual production. Tokyo Ohka needs to win a relevant qualification. The resulting demand must be genuinely additional, rather than production simply shifting from one country to another. The sales must then generate attractive cash flow after the expense of supplying them.

I do not add an arbitrary valuation premium for government cooperation or for initiatives such as Pax Silica. If those relationships create value, I expect to see it eventually in customer wins, material volumes, margins and returns on capital.

## The figures that matter

Tokyo Ohka's recent operating performance has been strong.

| Metric | FY2025 actual | FY2026 company guidance |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Revenue | ¥237.0bn | ¥291.0bn |
| Operating profit | ¥47.4bn | ¥60.6bn |
| Operating margin | ~20.0% | ~20.8% |
| Earnings per share | ¥278.42 | ¥355.21 |

First-half 2026 revenue was approximately **¥139.7bn**, up **25.1%** year over year. Operating profit reached roughly **¥28.9bn**, up **45.4%**.

That is excellent operating growth. It does not settle the valuation question.

I am particularly concerned about the difference between reported earnings and actual cash generation. In 2025, operating cash flow minus cash payments for tangible and intangible investments was approximately **¥9.8bn**, compared with **¥47.4bn** of operating profit. The same cash-flow proxy was approximately **¥4.6bn** in 2024.

This does not mean the company is economically weak. It has been investing heavily in manufacturing capacity. The question is whether those investments lead to a higher level of sustainable cash generation.

The facilities now being completed provide a practical test. Tokyo Ohka finished its new Koriyama manufacturing building in October 2026, with production planned to begin in November. Its Pyeongtaek plant in South Korea was completed in September, with operations planned for the first half of 2027.

Those milestones matter much more to me than an isolated daily share-price move. Over the next several years I want to know what utilization, incremental margins and cash returns these facilities actually achieve.

## Price and expected return

The share price closed at **¥7,750 on October 27, 2026**. Using management's FY2026 EPS guidance of **¥355.21**, that is approximately **21.8 times** expected 2026 earnings.

I would not call that cheap without qualification.

My October research included a dated FactSet estimate snapshot for 2030 of approximately **¥466.2bn revenue**, **¥108.8bn operating profit**, **¥650.17 EPS** and **¥68.6bn free cash flow**. Those are external forecasts recorded at that date, not management guidance and not guaranteed outcomes. The precise cash-flow definition needs additional reconciliation before I can treat it as fully consolidated unlevered free cash flow.

For a simple earnings-based valuation, I assume Tokyo Ohka earns **¥650.17 per share in 2030**, trades at **18 times earnings**, and delivers approximately **¥524** in cumulative modeled dividends. That gives a terminal wealth estimate near **¥12,227 per share**.

Relative to a ¥7,750 entry price, that works out to about **11.5% annually** over the model's approximate 4.2-year period.

The dividend total is a simplified end-wealth assumption; some distributions may be paid later than the model's nominal end date. It excludes foreign exchange, taxes, transaction costs and reinvestment timing.

I have also kept a less comfortable set of scenarios. The exact probabilities are judgment calls, not statistically calibrated estimates.

| Case | End value including modeled dividends | Approximate annual return from ¥7,750 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Severe bear | ¥3,780 | −15.7% |
| Bear | ¥5,860 | −6.4% |
| Base | ¥12,227 | +11.5% |
| Bull | ¥18,200 | +22.5% |

Using my original illustrative weights of 10%, 25%, 45% and 20%, the weighted terminal value is approximately **¥10,985**, or **8.7% annualized** from the current reference price.

That is not an especially large expected advantage over a demanding 8% annual benchmark assumption. It also ignores yen movements. I will not describe it as proven alpha.

The discounted-cash-flow work is more sobering. Updating the October 21 market enterprise-value estimate for the lower share price produces an approximate **¥901bn market EV**, using the previously reported share count and balance-sheet bridge. My cash-flow model values the company at roughly **¥925bn** with 8.5% capital costs and 2% perpetual growth, but only **¥854bn** at 9% and 2%.

A half-percentage-point change in the assumed cost of capital therefore changes the valuation conclusion.

The model has real limitations, especially its use of a forecast free-cash-flow series that still needs to be reconciled to the enterprise-value claim, including minority interests and investment requirements. I treat it as a sensitivity test, not a precise appraisal.

At the current price, the stronger investment case needs evidence of sustainable cash flow eventually exceeding the approximately **¥68.6bn** estimate for 2030. Under the 9% discount-rate test, the market price points toward a requirement around **¥73bn** in sustainable 2030 cash flow, provided the earlier-year path and other assumptions hold. A believable route to **¥80–85bn** without another equally large wave of investment would change my assessment materially.

That is where I think a meaningful positive surprise could come from.

## How this could go wrong

My main concern is not that semiconductors stop being important. It is that their importance fails to translate into the cash flow today's shareholders are paying for.

Tokyo Ohka might lose valuable qualifications as customers adopt different resist or packaging technologies. Its new facilities could take longer to fill than expected. More manufacturing capacity across the industry could depress demand or pricing in parts of its portfolio. Customers may extract a greater share of the economics than materials suppliers.

The valuation can also work against me. Even if earnings rise, the share price can disappoint if the market decides that 18 times future earnings is too generous. Higher capital spending or weaker cash conversion could make the apparently reasonable earnings multiple misleading.

There is a currency problem as well. The business is valued in yen, while I judge much of the portfolio against dollar-denominated alternatives. A weaker yen could erase part of an otherwise positive local-currency return.

These risks reinforce one another. A downturn that reduces factory utilization can lower earnings expectations, delay free-cash-flow recovery and compress the valuation multiple at the same time.

I would not be surprised by a very large drawdown in this kind of stock. The severe-bear model represents roughly **51% downside in terminal wealth** from the reference price, before an adverse currency effect. It is a stress scenario, not a measured probability or the maximum possible loss.

## Risk assessment versus my existing holdings

**Investment risk: 4/5 — high.**

I am using the same five-point scale as the other investment decisions in this repository. This is an assessment of downside and uncertainty for an equity investor, not a statement that Tokyo Ohka is financially close to failure.

The comparison matters because I do not want to classify every business with a good moat as low-risk.

| Holding | Relative investment risk | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mastercard | Lower than TOK | More established cash-flow profile and less exposure to semiconductor manufacturing cycles |
| Microsoft | Lower than TOK | Broader recurring revenue and less dependence on individual industrial capacity ramps |
| Synopsys | 4/5 | Technically entrenched business, but meaningful valuation, integration and technology-transition risks |
| **Tokyo Ohka Kogyo** | **4/5** | Strong qualified-materials position, offset by semiconductor cycles, process substitution and heavy investment needs |
| Micron | 5/5 | Much greater direct exposure to memory selling prices and the risk of peak-cycle earnings |
| Ambiq | Very high | Smaller operating scale and greater commercialization and execution uncertainty |

My October 14 archive rated both Synopsys and the subsequently exited Cadence at **4/5**. Micron was assigned **5/5** on October 26. Those are the fixed comparisons; the other relative descriptions are qualitative rather than an attempt to invent historical numerical scores.

Tokyo Ohka is not Micron. Its specialty materials business does not depend on DRAM and NAND pricing in quite the same way. Its established industrial position also makes it a different proposition from a much smaller semiconductor company such as Ambiq.

At the same time, Tokyo Ohka is less predictable than a diversified software or payments business. Its moat must survive technological transitions, and a substantial amount of its prospective value depends on getting acceptable returns from new capital.

The €6,700 allocation reflects that balance. It is meaningful enough to follow the thesis as an actual virtual portfolio decision, but deliberately below several of my larger core-company allocations. I am not using the previous €7,500 figure, and I am not pretending that a slightly smaller position makes the company itself safer.

There is also correlated portfolio risk. I already have exposure to semiconductor-related economics through Synopsys, Micron and Ambiq. They operate in different segments, but a reversal in AI infrastructure spending or semiconductor expectations could affect all of them. Adding Tokyo Ohka creates business-model diversification within that theme, not independence from the theme.

## What would change my mind

The next scheduled Q3 announcement is **November 10, 2026**. I will pay attention to demand, mix, margins, capital spending and any evidence about qualification wins. One strong quarter would not be sufficient proof.

Over time, I would increase confidence if the new plants ramp on schedule, high-value materials gain process share, and operating cash flow grows faster than the capital needed to support it. The most valuable evidence would be stronger sustainable free cash flow and higher incremental returns on invested capital than today's consensus implies.

I would reduce confidence if Tokyo Ohka loses important next-generation resist qualifications, if additional facilities remain underutilized, or if years of revenue and accounting profit growth fail to produce substantially better shareholder cash flow.

I also intend to reassess the stock against other opportunities. A good business is not automatically the best use of the next euro.

## Final decision

I am recording a **€6,700 virtual investment in Tokyo Ohka Kogyo**, with **4/5 investment risk**.

The reason is the possibility that a long-established Japanese semiconductor-materials supplier can apply its specialized chemistry and process expertise to an expanding set of valuable manufacturing steps, creating better incremental cash returns than the market expects.

The reason for restraint is that the current valuation already reflects considerable growth. My own models do not yet show a large, robust margin of safety, and the relationship between operating profit, capital expenditure and eventual free cash flow remains the central unresolved issue.

I think there is enough substance in the business to take a measured position. I do not think there is enough certainty to treat the stock as a low-risk compounder or assume that AI-related demand guarantees market-beating returns.

The bet is on better capital productivity and cash conversion, not simply on more semiconductor spending.
