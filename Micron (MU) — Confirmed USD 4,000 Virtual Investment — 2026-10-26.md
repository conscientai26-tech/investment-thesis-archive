## Why I bought it

The investment is a deliberately limited position in a possible structural change to the economics of memory, not a bet that memory prices can only rise.

AI infrastructure requires not just more compute but more data movement and memory capacity. High-bandwidth memory (HBM) allows advanced accelerators to access large volumes of data at sufficient speed; conventional server DRAM and NAND are also needed as inference workloads scale. The possible structural change is that memory becomes a more persistent constraint on AI deployment than it was in previous consumer-electronics cycles. HBM production also competes for scarce fabrication resources with other DRAM products. If demand grows faster than economically usable output, scarcity may extend to more of Micron's portfolio.

I am not assuming that every new AI device automatically adds the same amount of memory demand. Software efficiency, model architecture, compression, memory sharing and changing inference patterns can reduce memory per task. The relevant relationship is **total deployed workloads × memory required per workload**, compared with **industry-wide effective supply**. Personal AI, continuously active agents and local inference are *additional hypotheses*, not revenues I count as already earned.

The potential mispricing is in **duration**, rather than recognition, of the current boom. Markets already know about AI demand. For the stock to outperform, Micron must retain stronger pricing, mix and cash-generation economics for longer than the market has discounted. Long-term customer arrangements and HBM allocation may dampen some historical cyclicality, but neither makes industry pricing immune to oversupply.

## Why the upside could be substantial

Micron reported **$133.188 billion of FY2026 revenue, 80.7% GAAP gross margin and $84.969 billion of GAAP net income**. These are extraordinary outcomes for a memory manufacturer. The question is how much of that profitability represents a sustainable step change rather than unusually favorable prices. [Micron FY2026 results](https://investors.micron.com/financials/quarterly-results/default.aspx).

The economic leverage is clear. Once a fab is built, a higher realized price per memory bit can raise gross profit disproportionately because a substantial part of the production cost base does not fall or rise in step with selling prices. Better HBM product mix, higher yields and successful capacity allocation could reinforce this effect. If cloud inference, model-serving demand and on-device computing absorb new memory capacity faster than supply ramps, revenue and free cash flow could exceed the market's *post-boom* expectations.

Conversely, a low forward earnings multiple is **not itself evidence of cheapness**. Earnings estimates constructed near a pricing peak can make a cyclical stock appear exceptionally inexpensive shortly before profit normalization. My bullish case needs sustained, after-tax **free cash flow per share**, not simply strong forecast EPS for 2027–2028.

For context, the existing research dossier's two-year share-price illustrations from $877.55 are approximately **$427 in a sharp downturn, $873 under normalization, $1,458 in a structurally strong environment and $1,920 if the boom persists**. These are internally chosen scenarios, **not price targets validated by a calibrated probability model**.

## Why the stock could fall sharply

**1. Supply and selling prices are the central danger.** Strong profits finance new fabs and process upgrades at Micron and its competitors. When that capacity becomes productive, industry supply may grow faster than demand. The stock can then fall **even while memory bit shipments continue growing**. Price deterioration erodes gross margins much faster than headline AI demand growth would suggest. This is why an established memory producer still has risk that is fundamentally different from a sticky enterprise-software franchise.

History makes the downside concrete: from FY2022 to FY2023, Micron's revenue fell from **$30.758 billion to $15.540 billion** (about **−49.5%**); gross margin fell from **45.2% to −9.1%**; GAAP net income moved from **+$8.687 billion to −$5.833 billion**. That episode is not a forecast of the next cycle, but it disproves the idea that memory demand growth guarantees earnings stability. [Micron FY2023 results](https://investors.micron.com/news/press-release/2023/Micron-Technology-Inc--Reports-Results-for-the-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-of-Fiscal-2023-09-27-2023/default.aspx).

**2. Capital expenditure and cash-tax timing matter.** The FY2026 cash-flow statement reported **$89.675 billion of operating cash flow**, while Micron's **adjusted FCF was $62.31 billion** after its net-CapEx definition. Actual income taxes paid were only **$1.254 billion**, versus **$15.234 billion of current income-tax expense**. This difference is not a known tax bill due next year, but it means extrapolating current cash conversion without modeling future cash taxes would be unsafe. Rising fabrication investment, inventory or receivables could likewise consume cash even when accounting profits look excellent. [Micron FY2026 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/723125/000072312526000023/mu-20260903.htm).

**3. Contracts reduce uncertainty, not eliminate the cycle.** Customer commitments can help visibility of volume and some prices, but their disclosed scope and pricing terms must be respected. I will not treat all contracted revenue as guaranteed profit, assume uniform price floors or infer that every HBM contract insulates conventional DRAM and NAND.

**4. Valuation and expectation risk remain unusually high.** The market already discounts tremendous earnings growth. If investors become convinced that FY2027–2028 represents peak profitability, both future earnings expectations and the multiple applied to them can fall. A decline of 50–70% is a meaningful *stress scenario*, not my base forecast or an empirically estimated likelihood.

The allocation is intentionally smaller than an earlier $6,750 discussion because positive expected alpha has not been demonstrated robustly enough for a larger commitment. In a *hypothetical* $100,000 equity portfolio it would represent 4%; the archive has also used other reference-account sizes, so no actual portfolio weight should be asserted without reconciling current holdings.

At $4,000 committed capital, a 30% share-price gain contributes **+$1,200**, while a 70% decline would cost **−$2,800**, ignoring fees, dividends and FX. A smaller allocation contains the damage from an adverse cycle; it does **not** improve Micron's expected return.

## What would validate or invalidate the thesis

The decisive evidence is not another announcement that AI is growing. I will monitor **DRAM and NAND selling prices per bit versus bit shipments**, HBM yield and share of wafer capacity, competitor capacity additions, customer-contract repricing, and operating cash flow after actual cash taxes and total capital spending. These quantities should establish whether incremental memory demand can absorb industry supply without destroying margins.

I would strengthen the thesis if realized pricing remains resilient as new supply arrives, HBM remains capacity-constrained with attractive economics, and **normalized free cash flow per share remains strong after both taxes and sustained reinvestment**. I would weaken it if memory prices start falling despite healthy volumes, competitors expand capacity faster than demand, pricing protection proves narrower than expected or the market begins to price FY2026–2028 earnings as unsustainable.

**Decision conclusion:** I have **officially executed a $4,000 virtual Micron position on October 26, 2026** because a durable AI-driven change in memory scarcity is plausible and could be undervalued over a multi-year horizon. I accept that this proposition is uncertain, that the stock can suffer an exceptionally severe drawdown, and that the existing probability-weighted analysis does **not** prove positive alpha against the S&P 500. The modest position size is the expression of that uncertainty, not an excuse to ignore it.
