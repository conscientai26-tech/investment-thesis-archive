# The Root Moat, Reinvestment, and Expectations Engine
## A decision framework for finding businesses whose economic advantages can create *unexpected* shareholder value

**Date:** 7 November 2026  
**Status:** Research framework / testable methodology, not a demonstrated alpha-producing strategy  
**Relationship to existing work:** A focused, operational extension of [The Architecture of Alpha](./The%20Architecture%20of%20Alpha%20%E2%80%94%20An%20Investment%20Philosophy%20%E2%80%94%202026-11-07.md). It does not replace the broader philosophy of bottleneck migration, economic power, expectation gaps, risk management, and capital allocation.

---

## Executive thesis

A wide moat is a property of a *business*. Alpha is a property of an *investment at a particular price and time*. These are not interchangeable.

The objective is not simply to buy businesses with strong brands, high margins, low churn, or high historical returns on capital. It is to find companies for which a defensible causal advantage can generate more **incremental, per-share distributable cash flow** than the market already requires, for longer than the market assumes, at a purchase price that leaves adequate compensation for uncertainty and portfolio risk.

The proposed investment chain is:

**Structural change → root economic advantage → verified economic rents → incremental reinvestment opportunities → durable per-share cash generation → market-implied expectations → differentiated forecast → expected excess return → position sizing → monitoring and revision.**

Every arrow requires its own evidence. A compelling narrative at an earlier stage cannot substitute for data at a later one.

### The seven decision gates

1. **Root advantage:** Identify a concrete mechanism that competitors cannot economically and quickly replicate, neutralize, or bypass.
2. **Real economic profitability:** Establish returns above the appropriate opportunity cost of capital, after necessary reinvestment and accounting normalization.
3. **Incremental economics:** Establish that the *next* dollar invested can earn attractive returns, not merely that investments made long ago were profitable.
4. **Reinvestment runway:** Determine how much capital can be deployed at those attractive incremental returns, over how many years, and under what competitive constraints.
5. **Price and expectations:** Reverse-engineer what operating trajectory and duration the current share price requires. Compare it with an independently constructed distribution of outcomes.
6. **Survival and portfolio fit:** Test failure modes, financing constraints, correlation with existing positions, liquidity, valuation sensitivity, and permanently impaired downside.
7. **Observable disconfirmation:** Record measurable indicators and review dates that can invalidate the causal mechanism or alter expected returns before the next major market repricing.

**A great business can fail gate 5; a cheap business can fail gates 1–4; a compelling isolated investment can fail gate 6.**

## I. Identify the root moat, not a pile of correlated symptoms

The five conventional families are **switching costs, network effects, intangible assets, structural cost advantage, and efficient scale**. They are useful categories, not five independent points to award automatically.

The analysis must form a causal graph:

- **Mechanism:** What exact economic advantage makes replacement unattractive or makes duplication uneconomic?
- **Customer or supplier behavior:** How does the mechanism change choice, price sensitivity, usage, customer acquisition, retention, or negotiating power?
- **Competitive response:** Why can a capable rival not reproduce the outcome at acceptable cost and within the relevant time?
- **Economic capture:** What part of the benefit survives as revenue, operating profit, and cash after competitors, customers, suppliers, and distributors bargain for their shares?
- **Observable proof:** Which external or internal measures would change if the mechanism were real or weakening?

**Avoid double-counting.** An ERP company's retention, pricing power, recurring revenue, and margins may all trace back to a single migration-cost mechanism. Four observations can support one cause; they do not automatically establish four separate sources of protection.

**The replacement test:** Imagine a technically competent rival with capital, access to modern AI tools, and permission to price aggressively. List everything it would need to replace the incumbent's customer benefit. For each barrier, state (a) the cost, (b) time, (c) uncertainty, (d) scale dependence, (e) who controls it, and (f) the route by which the barrier might disappear.

**The bypass test:** Even if the rival cannot replicate the incumbent's product, can it make the product unnecessary? This is crucial for software, search, payments, data, and distribution. Disintermediation can destroy economic rents without copying the original asset.

**The dependency test:** A network effect that depends on a dominant external app store or data channel is not equivalent to an independently controlled network. Distinguish owned infrastructure from rented access, revocable permissions, and economically dependent partners.

**The independent-proof test:** Seek multiple indicators that originate from different causal measurements. Churn, retention, and net revenue retention from the same cohort are related; independent price/volume experiments, customer switching behavior, partner take rates, and new-entrant unit economics provide stronger triangulation.

### A falsifiable moat dossier

For each company, record:

| Field | Required conclusion |
| --- | --- |
| Root mechanism | One-sentence causal mechanism, not a descriptive label |
| Beneficiary and payer | Who receives value, and who ultimately funds the rent |
| Moat owner | Which economic actor actually controls the constraint |
| Rival replication path | Best realistic competitor's shortest response |
| Bypass/disruption path | How new architecture could remove the need for the incumbent |
| Unit-level evidence | Retention, price, volume, margin, cost, or customer-return data |
| Strength and persistence | Magnitude **and** probable duration, with uncertainty |
| Counterevidence | Strongest available falsifying observation |
| Conditions for migration/expansion | Necessary steps if the advantage must adapt to technological change |
| Review trigger | Leading observable warning, with date/frequency |

Avoid claiming "wide moat" as a verified fact solely because an external rater uses that term. State what the underlying mechanism and supporting evidence actually are.

## II. Verify economic profit, with attention to accounting distortions

A moat must ultimately allow **returns on invested capital in excess of the risk-adjusted cost of capital** over a defensible horizon.

An illustrative operating measure is:

**ROIC = normalized NOPAT / normalized operating invested capital**, where NOPAT represents after-tax operating profit.

Economic spread:

**Economic spread = ROIC − WACC.**

Economic profit in a given period is approximately:

**Economic profit = invested capital × (ROIC − WACC).**

These expressions are analytical tools, not automatic answers. Different sectors and accounting policies require different treatment.

Audit at least five distortions:

1. **Intangibles:** R&D and customer-acquisition investments often pass through expenses even when they create multi-year assets. Compare reported and economically adjusted ROIC; do not capitalize costs indiscriminately.
2. **Acquisitions and goodwill:** Separate organic investment economics from purchased growth and accounting write-downs.
3. **Working capital and operating leases:** Normalize operating capital for the business model; check whether supplier financing flatters reported cash conversion.
4. **One-off margins and cycles:** Strip out temporarily favorable pricing, inventory accounting, restructuring, and peak-cycle operating margins.
5. **Shareholder economics:** Track free cash flow *per fully diluted share*, stock-based compensation, dilution, debt service, and whether buybacks truly reduce shares outstanding.

**Do not make 15% or 20% absolute ROIC a universal rule.** The relevant comparison is sector-appropriate normalized returns, sustainable *incremental* economics, risk, and cost of capital. A firm may create substantial value at 12% if its cost of capital is 8%, and destroy value at 18% if its true economic hurdle is higher.

A reported high ROIC on a tiny denominator is a prompt for investigation, not automatic proof of a moat.

### Operating terminology and economic interpretation

**Switching costs** are the monetary, technical, organizational, contractual, time, and execution-risk burdens a customer would incur by replacing a supplier. They are not limited to cancellation fees. In enterprise software, the meaningful costs may be migrating historical data, rebuilding integrations and security controls, retraining employees, interrupting established workflows, and accepting the possibility of operational failure. A lower subscription price is not necessarily enough to overcome these costs. The relevant analytical question is whether a capable alternative can now reduce *total switching cost*, for example using AI-driven migration, interoperability, or a different workflow architecture.

**Operating activity** refers to the ordinary production and sale of a company's goods and services, rather than financing decisions or unrelated gains. **Operating income**, often expressed as EBIT (earnings before interest and taxes), is revenue minus operating costs such as cost of goods sold, sales, R&D, administration, depreciation, and amortization, under the relevant reporting standard. For a stylized business with $10 billion of revenue, $5 billion of production and personnel costs, and $2 billion of other operating expenses, EBIT is $3 billion. This is an accounting illustration, not a cash-flow figure.

**NOPAT (net operating profit after taxes)** is a normalized measure of after-tax operating profit, usually approximated by adjusted EBIT multiplied by (1 − a suitable operating tax rate). It is designed to isolate operating earning power from capital structure. The choice of adjustments, tax rate, operating leases, intangible investments, and invested-capital definitions can materially affect measured ROIC; they must be disclosed and held consistent across the numerator and denominator.

**Invested capital** is capital committed to the operations that generate NOPAT—not simply the company's market capitalization or the money a stock buyer pays for its shares. **Free cash flow** additionally depends on reinvestment, working capital, and cash expenses. Higher NOPAT therefore does not necessarily mean an equal increase in immediately distributable cash.

**Why switching costs matter to returns:** A migration-cost moat can allow renewal retention, lower acquisition costs, or pricing resilience, which in turn may protect NOPAT and incremental returns. But customer retention, recurring revenue, and high margins can all be consequences of the *same* switching-cost mechanism; treating them as independent moats would overstate confidence.

## III. The critical upgrade: returns on *new* capital

Historical ROIC measures the performance of the invested base. Shareholder value from expansion depends on marginal returns.

An approximate incremental-return diagnostic is:

**Incremental ROIC ≈ change in normalized NOPAT over a suitable interval / cumulative incremental invested capital attributable to that interval.**

Use operating cohorts, project-level data, product economics, multi-year changes, or unit-level economics when available. A simple one-year change in profit divided by one-year change in assets can be misleading because of investment lags, cyclicality, M&A, intangible expensing, and denominator noise.

Separate at least three sources of future per-share value:

1. **Defending the existing franchise:** How much capital and expense must be spent merely to prevent shrinkage?
2. **Organic reinvestment:** How much *additional* capital can be deployed into expansion at an attractive *incremental* return?
3. **Capital returned or redeployed:** What happens to surplus capital—cash retention, buybacks, dividends, debt reduction, or acquisitions—and at what price/return?

The distinction matters because a company earning 30% on historical capital might have almost no attractive projects left, whereas one earning 18% on its current base might be able to reinvest substantial amounts for many years.

### Reinvestment capacity is as important as reinvestment efficiency

Estimate the annual **reinvestment opportunity set**: market demand that can be profitably addressed, physical or operational capacity, customer acquisition costs, distribution reach, competitive supply response, and capital required per additional unit of output.

Track the *direction* of marginal economics: ROIIC stable, expanding, or declining as the company grows?

For software and platforms, calculate whether growth requires increasing distribution spending, discounts, sales effort, model-serving expenses, partner revenue shares, or customer-support costs. For hardware and infrastructure, include working capital, capex, replacement cycles, energy, permitting, and funding costs.

When profitable reinvestment opportunities are exhausted, value depends increasingly on capital distribution discipline and the price paid for the business—not on extrapolating previous growth.

### Practical ROIIC measurement: from accounting data to a credible forward forecast

The question is **how much additional normalized after-tax operating profit will be created by an identifiable increment of capital, and when?** Historical capital productivity is a clue, not a guarantee that future projects will reproduce it.

**A simple illustrative calculation.** Suppose a company has $10 billion invested capital and $2 billion annual NOPAT: historical ROIC is 20%. It invests another $5 billion. Once the projects have matured, normalized NOPAT rises to $2.75 billion. If the $750 million improvement is attributable to that investment, the estimated incremental ROIC is **$0.75bn / $5bn = 15%**. If it instead generates only $300 million, ROIIC is **6%**. At an illustrative 8% cost of capital, the 15% project creates an economic surplus while the 6% project fails that hurdle; neither percentage alone determines stock attractiveness.

#### Five complementary evidence tests

**1. Trace investment to results over a suitable interval.** Reconstruct changes in normalized operating invested capital and NOPAT using several years, preferably rolling three- to five-year windows when the business cycle and disclosure support them. Align incremental capital deployed in earlier years with operating profits earned *after* the relevant deployment lag; compare alternative lag specifications and explain why they are economically appropriate. A one-year ratio can become meaningless if the capital denominator barely moves, becomes negative, includes a large acquisition, or precedes the eventual output. Report raw and adjusted results.

**2. Decompose the investment itself.** Separate maintenance spending needed to preserve the existing business, organic expansion, intangible investment through R&D or customer acquisition, acquisitions, and capital returned to shareholders. Capex reported in the cash-flow statement does not capture all economic investment in software businesses. Conversely, not every R&D dollar necessarily creates a valuable, durable asset. A defensible capitalization adjustment requires realistic benefit lives, amortization assumptions, and sensitivity tests. Distinguish acquisition price from organic capital deployed.

**3. Verify project, product, and customer-cohort economics.** Where disclosed, evaluate additional capital, unit volume, realized price, utilization, contribution margin, renewal behavior, and incremental operating costs at the project or cohort level. For data centers, relate capex to contracted usable capacity, power, utilization, energy and maintenance costs. For enterprise software, relate acquisition/development spending to cohort gross profit, retention, support, migration costs, and expansion revenue. For advertising platforms, examine advertiser acquisition, campaign profitability, publisher distribution cost, serving costs, and independent evidence of conversion lift. **Revenue growth is not by itself incremental return on capital.**

**4. Test the amount of profitable demand and competitors' response simultaneously.** Estimate customer demand at profitable prices *and* competitor supply additions, substitution, pricing pressure, regulatory and platform constraints, and industry-wide financing. A supply-constrained semiconductor producer can display excellent ROIIC today but face declining incremental pricing power when rival factories enter service. A software vendor can appear to have nearly zero marginal production cost while acquiring later customer cohorts becomes progressively more expensive.

**5. Forecast a range of *incremental* outcomes, and update it.** Model the next tranche of reinvestment using coherent revenue, operating-margin, working-capital, capex, and deployment-timing assumptions. For a hypothetical $5 billion tranche, scenarios of **6%, 15%, and 25%** incremental ROIC correspond to $300 million, $750 million, and $1.25 billion of steady-state incremental annual NOPAT respectively. These are illustrative return scenarios, **not probabilities or forecasts for a real issuer**. Specify what demand, pricing, cost, and timing would produce each one. Then link the resulting per-share owner cash flow to the market-implied valuation and benchmark return.

#### A key attribution problem: old projects can conceal new-project economics

The aggregate change in NOPAT need not equal the profit from new investments:

- **Downward bias:** The incumbent franchise loses $400 million of annual NOPAT while a new $5 billion program produces $750 million. Consolidated NOPAT rises by only $350 million. A naive estimate yields 7% even though the new program generated 15%, before questions of maintenance spending and shared costs.
- **Upward bias:** Old products gain $500 million through a cyclical pricing spike while a new $5 billion program produces only $250 million. Consolidated NOPAT rises by $750 million, implying a misleading 15% when the new program generated 5%.

Therefore separate, where possible, **legacy-business price/volume/margin effects**, **new investment cohorts**, **acquisitions and disposals**, **currency effects**, **one-offs**, and **changes in capital intensity**. Segment data, project filings, management disclosures, customer cohorts, and competitor economics are imperfect but useful triangulation sources. If that attribution is impossible, report ROIIC as a *consolidated proxy*, not a verified project-level return.

#### Are diminishing returns inevitable?

No fixed rule requires every company's marginal return to fall immediately or monotonically. The most profitable customers or locations can be exhausted, competitive supply can increase, and customer acquisition can become more expensive. But learning curves, reusable software, operational scale, improved technology, network effects, and complementary-product demand can temporarily sustain or increase marginal returns. The question is **which force dominates for the *next* increment of capital and for how long**. Even when ROIIC declines from 25% to 15%, reinvestment may continue to create economic value if risk-adjusted capital costs remain materially lower.

#### Required output in every substantive company analysis

Produce a dated **incremental-capital evidence sheet**:

| Item | Required entry |
| --- | --- |
| Incremental investment | Dollar amount, period, business segment, maintenance vs growth vs acquisitions |
| Profit attribution | Estimated NOPAT from new projects versus legacy margin/volume movements |
| Timing | Deployment date, commercial start, ramp and normalized maturity, alternative lags |
| Historical observations | At least several periods of normalized aggregate ROIIC, with explicit denominator limitations |
| New-cohort evidence | Unit economics, utilization, retention, customer acquisition, gross profit or contribution margin |
| Scale ceiling | Profitable market demand, physical/organizational capacity, competitive investment pipeline |
| Forward ROIIC range | Bear/base/bull incremental NOPAT, capital needs, timing, margins and uncertainty |
| Economic hurdle | Appropriate WACC or project risk-adjusted hurdle; verify sustainable spread |
| Price expectations | Market-implied incremental returns, duration and reinvestment runway |
| Break conditions | Leading signs of deteriorating demand, price, costs, supply, switching costs or funding |
| Evidence grade | Dated primary disclosure, third-party estimate, owner assumption, or model calculation |

**Research standard:** No single ratio reliably establishes future ROIIC. The strongest case combines historical investment-to-profit evidence, segment or cohort unit economics, an explicit addressable profitable opportunity set, a credible competitor-response model, and ongoing falsification. Label missing inputs rather than filling gaps with precision.

## IV. Model how the moat changes through time

A static moat label cannot capture structural change. Classify the likely trajectory:

- **Expansion:** Existing mechanisms become more valuable as customers, use cases, or complements grow.
- **Migration:** Management must transfer an advantage into a new technological or market architecture.
- **Erosion:** A rival, substitute, or industry change reduces pricing power, retention, or profitability.
- **Dependency failure:** A supplier, regulator, platform, financier, or customer removes a condition on which the moat depends.

Estimate *when* each scenario could occur, the leading indicators, and its effect on future cash flows. Do not add arbitrary numerical probabilities merely to make the model appear scientific.

This complements the broader investment philosophy's **scarcity migration, capital conversion, root-moat, expectation-anchor, and system-correlation** principles.

**The opportunity is strongest where the business's economic advantage is expanding faster—or eroding slower—than the market expects, and the gap is not yet priced.**

The weakest narrative is one requiring several uncertain events in sequence (successful product reinvention *and* lower customer churn *and* new distribution *and* financing relief *and* a higher valuation multiple). Multiplying favorable assumptions quickly creates fragile projections.

## V. Reverse the price before forecasting upside

Do not begin valuation by selecting a desirable target price. Start with the current enterprise and equity value, share count, debt-like claims, cash, and normalized owner cash flows.

Use a reverse discounted cash-flow or transparent per-share earnings/FCF model to ask:

- What annual cash growth is required for today's valuation to make sense?
- How long must returns on incremental investment remain above the hurdle rate?
- What terminal margins and reinvestment rates are implied?
- What competitive assumptions must hold?
- How much of the optimistic thesis is *already in the price*?

Then construct independent downside, central, and upside cases, each with *one coherent bundle* of revenue growth, margin, reinvestment, dilution, competitive outcomes, and terminal valuation. Avoid assuming both unlimited growth and an undiminished premium multiple without explanation.

**The expectation gap is not simply a difference between your forecast and analyst consensus.** Consensus estimates are one observable proxy; the traded price may embed a different distribution of views, institutional positioning, discount rates, and risk premiums.

A coherent potential alpha calculation should compare the scenario distribution of **total shareholder returns after dilution, distributions, and costs** with the **total return of a benchmark or competing investment over the same dates**, not compare stock price performance with an inconsistent benchmark basis.

For scenario s over T years, an illustrative annualized relative-return spread is:

**Alpha_s = [(terminal equity value per share + cumulative shareholder distributions per share) / initial price]^(1/T) − 1 − benchmark annualized total return_s.**

The formula is a simplified comparison. If distributions are paid at intermediate dates, use actual cash-flow timing or an appropriately time-weighted/IRR-consistent representation; do not lump them at the end without adjustment.

If probabilities are defensible, calculate a probability-weighted distribution of relative outcomes and examine the lower tail. Otherwise give transparent *break-even conditions and scenario ranges*, not invented expected alpha precision.

**The attractive stock is not necessarily the business with the strongest moat; it is one where probable future economic value and the price disagree enough to compensate for uncertainty, risk, and alternatives.**

## VI. Make the decision relative, not isolated

A qualifying business can still be an unsuitable portfolio addition.

Before purchase, document:

1. **Investment alternatives:** Why does this allocation beat holding cash, an index, or the best existing portfolio position at today's prices?
2. **Concentration:** What share of total portfolio loss in a common adverse scenario would this position contribute?
3. **Hidden thesis correlations:** Do multiple stocks depend on the same AI capex, cloud spending, credit conditions, advertising demand, or discount-rate assumptions?
4. **Uncertainty vs. volatility:** Which outcomes permanently impair the cash-generating franchise, as opposed to temporarily changing its quoted price?
5. **Liquidity and price:** What is the available market depth and realistic slippage in normal and distressed markets?
6. **Operational rules:** What information invalidates the thesis, triggers a size reduction, permits a re-entry, or justifies realizing gains when expected *remaining* alpha declines?

No numerical position-size rule should be represented as optimal without testing. The portfolio should be sized with reference to loss capacity, correlated exposures, confidence calibration, time horizon, and opportunity cost.

## VII. A practical research and monitoring sheet

Use a *decision memo*, not a generic stock description.

| Block | Minimum required evidence | Pass/fail question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Root moat | Mechanism + best rival/bypass case + behavioral evidence | Is the advantage causally identifiable? |
| 2. Economic returns | Normalized ROIC, WACC, capital base, cash conversion | Does the franchise create sustainable economic profit? |
| 3. New capital | ROIIC evidence, growth capex, intangible investment, time lags | Does future growth create additional value? |
| 4. Runway | Addressable *profitable* demand and capacity constraints | Can those economics scale for long enough? |
| 5. Moat change | Expansion/migration/erosion/dependency paths | What would break the mechanism first? |
| 6. Reverse expectations | Implied growth, margins, duration, valuation | Is the necessary market expectation already aggressive? |
| 7. Return distribution | Bear/central/bull, total return versus benchmark | Is remaining alpha robust to reasonable input changes? |
| 8. Portfolio fit | Correlated exposures, risk of ruin, liquidity, alternatives | Is it better than the next-best use of capital? |
| 9. Monitoring | 3–5 leading indicators, source, frequency, decision threshold | Can the thesis be falsified in real time? |

**Minimum evidence discipline:** Mark each input as (a) verified primary source, (b) public third-party estimate, (c) owner assumption, or (d) model output. Record the source publication date and the date information became investable. Never retrospectively pass later outcomes into an earlier decision memo.

### Example of forward monitoring

A software company's purported switching-cost moat might be monitored through renewal cohorts, net retention, gross retention, contract length, realized prices, customer migration projects, deployment costs, support intensity, partner access, and the productivity of lower-cost substitutes. A fall in customer retention alone might be lagging evidence; increasing successful migrations to lower-cost rivals could be the leading causal signal.

An AI infrastructure supplier might be monitored through bottleneck utilization, competing capacity under construction, lead times, customer prepayments, capex requirements, debt pricing, and incremental returns—not only unit demand or revenue growth.

An advertising platform might be monitored through advertiser ROAS, customer acquisition, publisher access, take rate, platform dependence, and incrementality experiments—not only reported revenue or an asserted AI model edge.

## VIII. Decision rules: buy, hold, trim, sell, or do nothing

**Buy** only when the economic mechanism is sufficiently established, forward incremental economics and runway are attractive, price implies a weaker outcome than the independently supported range, and portfolio-level expected benefit justifies the downside.

**Hold** when the original causal mechanism remains valid and expected *remaining* excess return is still adequate. A past paper profit is not itself a reason to hold.

**Trim** when current valuation has captured substantial future upside, correlated portfolio risk has risen, or the downside has become too large relative to the marginal opportunity. Use an ex-ante policy; automatic +20% profit taking is a testable hypothesis, not a universal truth.

**Sell** when a key prerequisite fails, expected remaining return has become inferior to a realistic alternative after tax/costs, or risk breaches precommitted limits. The presence of a wide moat does not immunize a position from a rational exit.

**Do nothing** when data are inadequate, scenario outcomes are too sensitive to assumptions, price offers no margin for model error, or a superior capital allocation is unavailable. "No trade" is an active output of the process.

**Re-entry** is a separate valuation-and-evidence decision. Do not buy back merely because the price fell, and do not refuse to re-enter simply to avoid admitting an earlier sale was premature.

## IX. Test whether this framework actually improves results

This is not a backtest. Empirical validation remains necessary.

Build a historical universe *as known at each date*. Freeze data availability, published financials, analyst information, price, index membership, sector classification, and moat indicators at the decision timestamp. Apply realistic reporting lags; avoid survivorship, look-ahead, and restatement bias.

Compare, with matched universes and time windows:

- **Baseline A:** Broad, investable total-return benchmark.
- **Baseline B:** Quality alone (historically observable ROIC, without valuation).
- **Baseline C:** Quality plus reasonable valuation.
- **Model D:** Root moat + adjusted ROIC + incremental returns + runway.
- **Model E:** Model D plus reverse expectations, risk, and portfolio fit.

Measure portfolio **total-return alpha** with time-weighted and cash-flow-consistent methods as appropriate; compare factor exposures, drawdowns, volatility, concentration, turnover, transaction costs, taxes where relevant, and robustness across regimes. Use out-of-sample periods and make methodological and parameter choices before viewing the test results.

The test should answer the causal incremental question: **Does each added decision gate improve results relative to the simpler alternatives, or merely produce a more elaborate story?**

Historical wide-moat performance, an old benchmark chart, and a few exemplary companies do not establish this model's predictive alpha.

## X. The compressed investment question

> **Who controls the real economic advantage; how much *new* value can that advantage produce per share; how long can it persist; what must go right for that to happen; what is already priced in; and why is this investment superior to the alternatives after considering failure?**

When any part of the chain cannot be answered, that is an explicit uncertainty—not a gap to fill with confidence.
