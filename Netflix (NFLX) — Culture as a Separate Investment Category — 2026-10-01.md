# Culture as a Separate Investment Category — Netflix and the Problem of an Incomplete Category Model

**Date:** 2026-10-01  
**Context:** Follow-up to the original Netflix starter-position thesis from 2026-09-14

## The core lesson

The main lesson from Netflix is not that I failed to realize entertainment was a different category from my existing AI, infrastructure, and software investments.

That would be wrong.

My original Netflix thesis from September 14 already makes the difference explicit.

I wrote that entertainment was a newer field for me and that I understood AI infrastructure, enterprise software, cloud, bottlenecks, and capital conversion much better than film economics, franchise formation, and long-term entertainment behavior.

I also wrote:

> I do not want to confuse confidence in the company with confidence in my own model of the industry.

That is why the position was intentionally only $6,000.

I already described entertainment as:

**creative, probabilistic, and slow.**

I even identified one of the most important cultural realities:

**You cannot order cultural relevance with capital.**

So I did recognize that Netflix operated in a different world.

The mistake was one layer deeper.

---

## I recognized the category, but I did not model it deeply enough

After recognizing that entertainment was different, I quickly moved to the next question:

**Do my existing investment frameworks also work here?**

Netflix became a deliberate out-of-domain test of:

- Scarce-Core Expansion
- Catch-up Giant
- Bottleneck Asymmetry
- Deep / Railway Bottleneck
- Bottleneck Migration

That was intellectually useful, and some of those frameworks still fit Netflix surprisingly well.

But in hindsight I skipped an important intermediate step.

The better sequence should have been:

**1. Recognize the new category.  
2. Model the category independently.  
3. Identify its native mechanisms and risks.  
4. Only then apply existing investment frameworks.**

What I did was closer to:

**Recognize the new category  
→ test existing frameworks on it.**

That difference matters.

I knew I understood the industry less well.

But I had not yet built a sufficiently independent model of **how culture works as an economic system**.

---

## Risk management was better than the category model

Interestingly, my risk management was stronger than my analysis here.

In the original thesis I wrote:

> Because I like the thesis more than I trust my current entertainment model.

That is exactly why I kept the position small.

This means I correctly identified **model risk**.

I even partially priced that uncertainty into the position size.

What I had not done was fully reduce the model risk itself.

A smaller position protects me from being wrong.

It does not replace the understanding that is still missing.

That distinction is important.

---

## What the original Netflix thesis already got right

The original thesis already identified many of the correct risks.

I wrote that Netflix could lose attention to YouTube, creator platforms, gaming, and future AI-native entertainment.

I wrote that engagement could weaken while content spending continued to rise.

I recognized that Netflix does not have hard enterprise-style switching costs.

I recognized that durable cultural IP is difficult to create.

I recognized that Netflix can possess a strong distribution core while cultural relevance remains inherently non-deterministic.

Those ideas were not wrong.

What was missing was a deeper decomposition of the category itself.

---

## The speed finding shows exactly what was missing

A new variable became visible only after examining Netflix more directly as a cultural product:

**Netflix can be extremely fast at the company level and extremely slow at the individual-user level at the same time.**

Netflix releases a very large amount of content.

But an individual user cares about only a small fraction of it.

If someone loves *Wednesday*, ten new Korean dramas, several documentaries, and multiple romantic comedies do not necessarily solve that user's problem.

For Netflix, all of those titles count as new content.

For that user, Netflix may still feel as though it has produced almost nothing relevant for two years.

This creates a crucial distinction:

**Total content output ≠ personally relevant content output.**

---

## Production is slow, consumption is fast

High-quality scripted entertainment can take one to three years to produce.

Then a season is released.

Netflix still releases a large share of its original television seasons in binge form.

A user can therefore consume something that took years to produce in only a few days.

The cycle can look like this:

**Production: 1–3 years  
→ Release: all at once  
→ Consumption: a few days  
→ Waiting period: months or years again**

That is a structural asymmetry.

It exists because Netflix operates in the cultural category.

A Synopsys customer does not "finish" their design software.

A Microsoft user does not eventually "complete" Word.

Entertainment is different.

A specific piece of entertainment can be consumed and largely exhausted for a particular user.

---

## Personalized Content Replenishment Rate

This leads to a category-native variable that may matter more for Netflix than the raw number of productions:

# Personalized Content Replenishment Rate

The important question is not:

**How many new titles does Netflix release?**

The important question is:

**How quickly does an existing user receive something new that they genuinely want after consuming the content they already preferred?**

This variable connects:

- individual taste,
- production speed,
- production cycle length,
- binge release strategy,
- engagement,
- content spending,
- cultural relevance,
- competition for attention.

This is exactly the kind of variable I should have searched for before investing.

---

## Why my existing frameworks could not automatically reveal this

My strongest existing investment models were developed primarily through technical and infrastructure businesses.

There I usually ask:

- Where is the bottleneck?
- How deeply embedded is the product?
- How difficult is the supplier to replace?
- Where does scarcity migrate?
- Which adjacent capabilities can an incumbent expand into?
- Which technical dependency becomes more important over time?

Those are strong questions.

But they do not automatically generate questions such as:

**How quickly does personally relevant content get exhausted?**

Or:

**How often must a platform create a new cultural reason for the same user to return?**

Or:

**How different is total production speed from personal replenishment speed?**

Those questions emerge from the category itself.

That is why strong universal frameworks are not enough.

I also need to know when a new category requires additional tools.

---

## Culture is a separate investment category

Netflix has shown me something broader.

Culture has its own economic properties:

**Taste changes.**

**Attention is limited.**

**Novelty loses value through consumption.**

**Hits are only partially predictable.**

**Franchises take time to build.**

**Cultural relevance can appear quickly and disappear quickly.**

**Production can be slow while consumption is extremely fast.**

**Competition comes not only from direct competitors but from every alternative use of human leisure time.**

That is fundamentally different from many infrastructure or enterprise businesses.

Netflix does not only compete with Disney or HBO.

It competes with:

YouTube, TikTok, gaming, Twitch, cinema, podcasts, social media, creators, live events, and ultimately the decision to spend time on something else entirely.

Culture therefore needs its own model.

---

## The actual meta-lesson

The original Netflix thesis was not naive.

The instinct was correct:

**New industry → higher model uncertainty → smaller position.**

That was good risk management.

But the next step should have been:

**New industry → build a category model first.**

Not immediately:

**How well do my existing frameworks work here?**

That is the important methodological correction.

When I enter a new investment category, it is not enough to recognize that I know less.

It is not enough to reduce position size.

I also need to understand the category itself before I can claim to understand the company.

---

## New rule for future investments

When entering an unfamiliar category, the sequence should be:

### 1. Identify the category

What kind of economic system is this?

### 2. Analyze the category independently

What mechanisms determine success and failure here?

### 3. Search for category-native variables

What risks could my existing frameworks miss?

### 4. Analyze the company inside that world

How well does it control the mechanisms that actually matter in this category?

### 5. Only then overlay the universal frameworks

Only after that should I ask whether Scarce-Core Expansion, Catch-up Giant, Bottleneck Migration, or another existing framework also applies.

The universal frameworks should complement the category model.

They should not replace it.

---

## What Netflix means for my process

Netflix is therefore not merely a weak-performing position.

It is a useful out-of-domain test of my investment process.

My original question was:

**Do my five theses also work outside AI and technology?**

The better question now is:

**What must I understand before I even try to apply those theses to a new category?**

The answer is:

**The category itself.**

That is the layer that was missing.

---

## Final lesson

The lesson is not:

**I failed to realize Netflix was different.**

I did realize it.

The lesson is:

**I realized Netflix was different, but I did not follow that insight far enough to build an independent model of the category before applying my existing frameworks.**

I identified model risk and partially controlled it through a smaller position.

But I underestimated the next step:

**Do not merely accept model risk. Reduce it by studying the category itself.**

The Personalized Content Replenishment Rate is a good example of what that process can uncover.

That variable was not obvious inside my original AI-derived frameworks.

It only became visible once I stopped looking at Netflix primarily as a company with bottlenecks and started looking at it as part of a cultural system with its own laws.

The new rule is therefore:

> **When I enter an unfamiliar investment category, I must first learn how that world works. Only then should I believe that I understand the company.**
