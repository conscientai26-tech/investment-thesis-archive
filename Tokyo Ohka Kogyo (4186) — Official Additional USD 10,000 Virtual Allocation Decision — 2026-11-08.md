## My decision

I am increasing my Tokyo Ohka position by another $10,000.

I originally recorded a €6,700 virtual position on October 27. Since then the stock has fallen hard. It closed at ¥7,750 on the date of the original entry, was at ¥8,014 on November 2, and finished November 6 at ¥6,404. That is about 17.4% below my earlier reference price and about 20.1% below the November 2 close. It is a material change in price over a very short period.

I am not buying simply because a stock has fallen 20%. A falling share price is not proof of a bargain. Sometimes the market is reacting faster than a fundamental analyst, and sometimes it is pricing a problem that has not yet appeared in reported financial statements. The question is whether the decline reflects a corresponding deterioration in the value of the business.

My working conclusion is that the evidence available before Q3 does not yet justify assuming that Tokyo Ohka's underlying economics have deteriorated as quickly as the share price. That is a judgment, not an established fact. I want the position because I believe the business has a credible path to making more cash than the current valuation implies. The lower entry price improves the possible return if that thesis holds.

This is an official decision for my tracked virtual portfolio. It is not a claim that an actual order has been filled or that the full updated position has a verified cost basis.

## What I think the market is missing

The AI investment boom is usually described through the companies building models, buying GPUs, operating data centers, or selling semiconductor equipment. I think an equally important question is which businesses supply components that cannot easily be removed from the manufacturing process even when the main technologies change.

Tokyo Ohka belongs to the latter category.

It sells photoresists and high-purity chemicals used in semiconductor fabrication, together with specialized materials used in packaging, MEMS, and wafer handling. Lithography is not an optional step in producing advanced logic and memory chips. Photoresists are one of the materials that allow microscopic patterns to be transferred reliably onto wafers. Small defects at the material level can become expensive production problems for a customer.

This matters economically because the price of the resist is only one part of the customer's calculation. A material that appears cheap compared with the cost of the fabrication facility can still be critical to yield. When a material has been qualified in a production process, replacing it is not the equivalent of switching a commodity supplier. The customer has to revalidate performance, consistency, contamination control and yield under real manufacturing conditions.

That creates switching costs. More precisely, it creates a form of process-level dependence: the supplier's chemistry, quality control and accumulated knowledge become embedded in how the customer's manufacturing process works.

I do not assume this makes Tokyo Ohka irreplaceable. It does not. There are formidable competitors, including JSR, Shin-Etsu and Fujifilm, and next-generation process technology can shift which resist chemistry is most valuable. A company can have strong retention in an existing generation and still lose the next one. My thesis is that Tokyo Ohka's accumulated qualifications, materials engineering and customer relationships give it a credible chance to expand alongside advanced chip manufacturing — not a guarantee that it wins every transition.

## Why this is a real operating business, not just an AI story

The first half of 2026 already produced strong results. Tokyo Ohka reported ¥139.668 billion in revenue, up 25.1% year on year, and ¥28.854 billion in operating profit, up 45.4%. Electronic functional materials grew 27.5%; high-purity chemicals grew 21.2%. The company identified stronger demand from AI servers as an important driver.

More revealing is the product mix. The August presentation reported approximately 35% year-on-year growth in advanced EUV/ArF resist materials, about 30% in KrF resist and approximately 25% in materials for back-end semiconductor processes in the first half. These are product-level growth figures, not growth rates for the entire company, but they establish that advanced manufacturing demand has already reached the supplier.

Management raised the full-year forecast on July 30. The August presentation gave FY2026 guidance of ¥291 billion revenue and ¥60.6 billion operating income, compared with ¥237.0 billion and ¥47.4 billion in 2025. Those are projected increases of 22.8% and 27.9%, respectively. Management's forecast for 2026 operating income had already surpassed its original 2027 medium-term target.

None of that guarantees a strong Q3 report on November 10. The earnings still have to confirm the production volumes, margins, product mix and outlook that investors now expect. But it means this is not a company whose valuation depends entirely on an imagined future market. It has already shown substantial commercial growth.

I am particularly interested in the different engines of that growth. Advanced lithography materials may grow from finer process requirements. High-purity chemicals can benefit from actual wafer processing activity. Packaging and wafer-handling materials can gain relevance as HBM, 2.5D and 3D integration become more important. These are connected to the semiconductor cycle but are not identical revenue streams. A disappointment in one does not automatically make every other one worthless.

The same applies in the other direction: I cannot multiply one fast-growing product's percentage across the whole business. To value Tokyo Ohka properly, I need actual segment weights, margins and future capital requirements.

## The part of the thesis that matters most: capital productivity

My general investment philosophy is that technological progress makes some resources abundant while making complementary constraints more economically important. More capable AI does not manufacture its own semiconductors. Higher compute demand still has to pass through fabrication capacity, precision materials, equipment, skilled production and capital.

Tokyo Ohka has an existing technical base in materials chemistry and process control. The upside is not merely that it will sell more chemicals. It is that its existing research, customer relationships, qualification experience and quality systems may become useful across more high-value manufacturing steps without requiring the same proportional increase in costs.

If revenue expands and additional operating profit requires a smaller proportionate amount of new capital, incremental return on invested capital should improve. That is where the long-term value would come from.

There is also an uncomfortable counterargument, and I do not want to disguise it. Tokyo Ohka is not a software company. It needs factories, purification systems, research facilities, inventory, working capital and highly reliable production. The company is spending heavily to expand. The August presentation projected ¥35.6 billion of capital investment for 2026, compared with ¥11.6 billion of depreciation. High reported profit can coexist with much weaker current free cash flow when a company is in an investment cycle.

My October analysis noted that a simple measure of 2025 operating cash flow less cash invested in tangible and intangible assets was roughly ¥9.8 billion, against ¥47.4 billion in operating profit. That gap is too important to ignore. It may reflect investment that will produce attractive future earnings, but it is not proof that those future cash returns will arrive.

The new Koriyama manufacturing building was completed in October 2026; the Pyeongtaek plant in South Korea was completed in September, with operations expected during the first half of 2027. Their utilization and eventual cash contribution will tell me much more than a single share-price move. A factory that is finished but underutilized is not an economic moat. A factory that reliably supplies materials customers cannot easily replace can be.

This is why the thesis must ultimately be tested through free cash flow, utilization, customer qualifications, pricing and incremental returns — not just the growth of accounting revenue.

## The lower stock price changes the investment calculation

The October 27 entry used a ¥7,750 share price. At that price, the company's FY2026 projected EPS of approximately ¥355.21 implied a forward P/E of about 21.8. At the November 6 close of ¥6,404, the same earnings estimate implies approximately 18.0 times.

That difference is meaningful. A lower multiple gives me more room for a good operating business to produce a satisfactory return. It does **not** prove that the stock is now cheap: the earnings estimate may itself be too high, and the market may be anticipating a downgrade.

My previous long-range illustration used an external 2030 EPS estimate of ¥650.17, an 18× terminal multiple, and approximately ¥524 of modeled cumulative dividends, resulting in around ¥12,227 of nominal terminal wealth per share. Those figures were dated assumptions, not forecasts that I can independently guarantee. At the new ¥6,404 reference price, that same terminal value implies roughly 1.91 times the purchase value before currency effects, taxes and fees.

The apparent improvement in expected return is obvious; the change in the probability of achieving that terminal value is not. If the price declined because future cash flows have genuinely become less likely, carrying over the old probabilities would be a mistake.

My original October scenario set included severe-bear, bear, base and bull terminal values of roughly ¥3,780, ¥5,860, ¥12,227 and ¥18,200. I am retaining these only as sensitivity anchors. They are not independently calibrated probabilities. The bear cases remain real even after the selloff. A lower purchase price reduces some downside, but does not abolish it.

In particular, a company can grow earnings for several years while its stock still underperforms because the market changes the multiple it is willing to pay. The other critical variable is cash conversion. If the company needs another long period of expensive capacity building, the EPS growth that appears attractive today may not accrue to shareholders as quickly as expected.

My conviction has therefore increased more in **the price being offered** than in **the certainty of the forecast**.

## Why I am prepared to add before the earnings release

Tokyo Ohka has scheduled its Q3 FY2026 results for **Tuesday, November 10, 2026 at 14:00 Japan time**, which is 06:00 CET.

I do not have evidence that the Q3 release will beat the market's expectations. A company can report solid revenue growth and still fall if investors wanted faster growth, stronger guidance, greater gross margins or better free cash flow. I am not treating an earnings announcement as a predictable catalyst.

The trade is really an asymmetry judgment: after the recent selloff, I think the price may be discounting a deterioration that is larger than the deterioration I can currently identify in the business. If the coming results show normal, durable demand and no major breakdown in margins or investment returns, the valuation could recover. If management reduces its forecasts or signals lost qualifications, weaker factory utilization, slowing demand or poor cash conversion, the market may have been right to lower the price.

Those two outcomes are both plausible. I am increasing exposure because the first remains credible at a substantially lower quoted price, not because I have discovered a way to forecast the market's immediate reaction to earnings.

The recent decline could also be linked to sector rotation, risk-off positioning, valuation compression or information I have not identified. I have not established a definitive causal explanation for the November 4–6 selloff. I do not want to retrospectively call it an overreaction without testing that claim against the results.

## What I would count as confirmation or disconfirmation

For Q3 I will be reading the report with a few specific questions in mind.

First, are advanced EUV/ArF and other high-value semiconductor materials continuing to show demand and adoption consistent with the August guidance? Second, are the revenue gains coming with healthy product mix and operating margins, rather than just a temporary currency benefit? Third, is management maintaining or revising its FY2026 targets, and what are the explanations for any revision? Fourth, is there fresh evidence on new customer qualifications, plant ramp-ups and HBM/packaging demand? Fifth, how much capital will be required before the increased revenue becomes sustainably higher free cash flow?

If the results are strong but the stock falls, I will compare the actual numbers with the expectations embedded in the valuation. If the results are weak but the stock rises, I will not treat the price reaction as proof that the business improved. The report and the market reaction are two different observations.

Over the next two to four years, the decisive indicator is whether the company converts its manufacturing investments into greater cash generation and attractive incremental returns. I would lose confidence if leading-edge materials are displaced in important production nodes, qualification wins stop translating into revenue, new capacity remains underutilized, margins deteriorate, or the business repeatedly reports strong accounting earnings without a corresponding rise in owner cash flow.

These are reasons to revisit the thesis, not mechanical stop-loss triggers.

## Why this is still a high-risk position

I have become increasingly conscious of how much historical alpha can affect my judgment. After building up a strong performance record, it is tempting to protect the number as though it were an asset in its own right. It isn't. What matters is whether I am allocating the capital I have now intelligently.

That does not mean accepting any risk. Buffett's point about avoiding permanent losses is relevant here. Protecting capital is not the same thing as refusing to invest. It means refusing situations where the downside can permanently damage my ability to invest in the future.

The ¥6,404 reference price does not eliminate downside risk. A 30% fall in the new $10,000 allocation would mean a $3,000 mark-to-market loss on that addition alone, ignoring currency movements. A 50% fall would mean $5,000. The full portfolio effect would depend on its actual value and on the exposure I already have to the semiconductor and AI investment cycle.

Tokyo Ohka is different from Micron, Synopsys or Ambiq, but their risks are not independent. A reduction in semiconductor spending, a widespread repricing of AI infrastructure or a problem at a major fabrication customer could pressure multiple holdings at once. Adding another semiconductor-linked company diversifies business models; it does not necessarily diversify the underlying macroeconomic shock.

I continue to classify Tokyo Ohka as **4/5 investment risk**. That is a rating of valuation, uncertainty, cyclical exposure and capital requirements, not a claim that the company is close to financial distress.

I also do not want the act of committing to an additional $10,000 to become an excuse to stop questioning the position. The commitment is a decision made using present evidence, not a declaration that the market must eventually agree with me.
