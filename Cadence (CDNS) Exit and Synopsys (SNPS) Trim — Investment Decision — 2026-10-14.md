# Cadence Exit and Synopsys Position Reduction
**Investment Decision & Research Journal**

**Date:** October 14, 2026  
**Portfolio:** Paper portfolio  
**Decision:** Sell Cadence entirely and reduce Synopsys to $7,000  
**Reference prices:** October 13, 2026  
**Primary benchmark:** S&P 500 Total Return  
**Investment horizon:** 3–5 years  
**Status:** Proposed virtual trades; execution not yet recorded

**Prior decision:** [September 8, 2026 EDA investment thesis](./Synopsys%20(SNPS)%20%26%20Cadence%20(CDNS)%20%E2%80%94%20EDA%20Bottleneck%20Investment%20Decision%20%E2%80%94%202026-09-08.md)

## 1. Why I'm making this decision

I'm selling Cadence and reducing my Synopsys position because I no longer believe the expected returns justify the amount of capital I have allocated to them.

My opinion of the underlying businesses hasn't deteriorated nearly as much as my assessment of their investment attractiveness.

Both companies remain extremely important to semiconductor design. They operate in a technically demanding industry, have deeply integrated products and benefit from high switching costs. As chips become more complex, demand for their software could continue growing for years.

I still believe that part of the thesis.

What I'm less convinced of is that today's stock prices leave enough room for that growth to translate into above-market returns.

There is a difference between predicting a company's future correctly and making money from that prediction. If the market already expects the same outcome, being right doesn't necessarily give me an advantage.

That's the central reason for this decision.

## 2. The original thesis

My original reasoning was relatively straightforward.

AI is increasing demand for computing power. More companies are developing specialized chips, and semiconductor architectures are becoming increasingly complex.

That should create additional demand for electronic design automation, verification and simulation.

Cadence and Synopsys are positioned to capture some of that demand because their software is already embedded in the semiconductor industry's development processes.

The weak point in this reasoning is the assumption that greater design activity necessarily produces proportionally higher EDA revenue.

AI could increase the number of chips being designed while simultaneously reducing the amount of work, software capacity or engineering time required per design.

New tools could also become commercially viable without replacing Cadence or Synopsys completely. Even partial competition could reduce pricing power.

In other words, I may be right about the direction of the semiconductor industry without being right about how much of its future economic value these two companies will capture.

I need to distinguish industry growth from shareholder returns.

## 3. Why I'm selling Cadence

Cadence is the clearer decision.

At approximately $334 per share, the stock trades at around 41 times management's projected 2026 adjusted earnings.

That valuation already assumes a considerable amount of future success.

Cadence could continue growing revenue at double-digit rates and still produce disappointing returns if investors become less willing to pay such a high multiple for its earnings.

My five-year scenario model produced the following estimates:

| Scenario | Weight | Annualized return |
|---|---:|---:|
| Bear | 30% | -8.3% |
| Base | 50% | +5.5% |
| Bull | 20% | +19.4% |

The scenario-weighted terminal-value result was approximately **5.9% annually**, compared with my assumed S&P 500 return of 7.5%.

That implies roughly **-1.6 percentage points of annual expected alpha**.

These are model estimates rather than statistically calibrated forecasts. The scenario weights and future valuation multiples remain uncertain.

Still, the exercise exposed something important: Cadence needs sustained growth and a relatively favorable valuation environment to generate returns that justify its current price.

I don't see a sufficiently large gap between what I expect and what the market appears to expect.

The company may continue performing exceptionally well. I simply don't think owning it at this price offers a compelling enough advantage over the alternatives.

I'm not selling because I expect the stock to collapse. I'm selling because I no longer have a strong reason to expect it to outperform.

## 4. Why I'm keeping $7,000 in Synopsys

Synopsys looks more attractive on valuation.

At approximately $495 per share, it trades around 26 times management's projected fiscal 2027 adjusted earnings.

Its acquisition of Ansys also gives it opportunities beyond traditional EDA software, particularly in simulation and broader engineering workflows.

If the integration works, Synopsys could benefit from a larger addressable market, cross-selling opportunities and operating leverage.

My five-year model estimates:

| Scenario | Weight | Annualized return |
|---|---:|---:|
| Bear | 30% | -7.9% |
| Base | 50% | +9.4% |
| Bull | 20% | +23.3% |

The scenario-weighted terminal-value result is approximately **9.2% annually**.

Relative to the same 7.5% S&P 500 assumption, that suggests around **+1.7 percentage points of expected annual alpha**.

This is better than Cadence, but not particularly convincing.

A small change in growth assumptions, integration costs, margins or future valuation multiples could eliminate that advantage.

There is also a significant difference between Synopsys's adjusted earnings and its reported earnings. Some adjustments reflect acquisition accounting, while stock-based compensation represents a genuine economic cost to shareholders.

That makes the long-term cash flow available per share more uncertain than the adjusted earnings forecast might suggest.

I don't want to abandon Synopsys entirely because I still see a plausible opportunity. But I don't have enough confidence in the valuation to justify maintaining the original position size.

Keeping $7,000 allows me to remain invested without committing as much capital to assumptions I cannot predict particularly well.

## 5. What I'm actually changing

| Synopsys | $13,808 | $7,000 |
| Available capital | $0 | $17,084 |

These figures use the October 13 reference prices and the September 8 recorded starting allocations of **$9,000 CDNS and $11,000 SNPS**. They are approximate, exclude fees, taxes and FX, and are not execution confirmations.

I am freeing approximately $17,084.

This isn't an attempt to predict a market correction. I want the capital available for opportunities where the expected return is more attractive and the underlying business is easier to evaluate.

There is an important weakness in this decision, however.

Holding cash does not automatically improve returns.

If I fail to find better investments, the capital could underperform both the S&P 500 and the stocks I sold.

The exit decision and the reinvestment decision therefore need to be evaluated separately.

Selling Cadence may be reasonable even if my subsequent allocation of the proceeds turns out to be poor.

## 6. What could make this decision wrong

The biggest risk is that I'm underestimating how long these companies can sustain high growth.

Cadence may continue compounding earnings at a rate that justifies its premium valuation. AI-driven semiconductor complexity could generate more paid EDA demand than I currently expect.

Synopsys may also integrate Ansys more successfully than my base case assumes, creating a broader and more profitable engineering software business.

If either company substantially exceeds expectations, reducing my exposure could prove expensive.

A second possibility is that my valuation model is too conservative.

Future earnings multiples are difficult to forecast. I may be placing too much weight on the possibility of multiple compression and not enough on the durability of these businesses.

The third risk is my own behavior after selling.

If I become impatient, buy weaker companies simply because I have available capital, or repeatedly move between stocks without a genuine valuation advantage, the rotation strategy could destroy the benefits of good stock selection.

These are real possibilities, and I want to track them rather than explain them away later.

## 8. What I want to learn from this decision

The immediate financial result matters, but it isn't the only reason I'm documenting the trade.

Over the next five years, I want to find out which parts of my investment process actually work.

I have several hypotheses about my own decision-making.

**First, I may be better at identifying attractive companies than knowing when to sell them.**

If Cadence and Synopsys consistently outperform after I reduce exposure, that would suggest my original stock selection was good but my exit decisions were poor.

**Second, my strongest investment ideas may deserve substantially more capital than my average ideas.**

I want to determine whether my initial confidence and assessment of business predictability actually contain useful information.

If the investments I considered highly predictable consistently outperform the ones I found difficult to assess, I should eventually become more selective.

**Third, capital rotation may be an important source of returns.**

It is possible that I identify mispriced companies reasonably well, but that much of the resulting alpha is realized relatively early.

If so, moving capital into new opportunities could improve long-term results.

It is equally possible that my best ideas compound for years and that selling them early reduces returns.

I don't know which explanation is correct yet.

That's what the experiment is supposed to establish.

## 10. The underlying competitive advantage

I also want to avoid counting several consequences of the same advantage as independent reasons to invest.

For Cadence and Synopsys, the underlying advantage is their position in technically demanding, difficult-to-replace industrial software workflows.

Recurring revenue, high customer retention, pricing power and profitability are partly consequences of that same position.

They are not necessarily four independent sources of competitive advantage.

The question I need to revisit is whether the underlying advantage itself is becoming stronger or weaker.

A company could maintain healthy revenue growth while its long-term competitive position gradually deteriorates.

The financial consequences might not become visible until years later.

For these two companies, the most important things to monitor are whether AI reduces the difficulty of replacing existing tools, whether customers gain negotiating power and whether new competitors become commercially credible.

That should tell me more about the durability of the business than simply watching quarterly revenue growth.

## 11. How I'll evaluate the sale

I will continue tracking Cadence and the portion of Synopsys I sold, even though they are no longer part of my active portfolio.

I want three separate records:

**Actual portfolio:** What happened to the capital after my decision?

**Shadow portfolio:** What would have happened if I had kept the original Cadence and Synopsys positions?

**Benchmark portfolio:** What would have happened if I had allocated the same capital to the S&P 500?

I'll compare the results after 30, 90, 180 and 365 days, then after two and five years.

The calculations should include dividends, transaction costs and any relevant currency effects. Taxes should be tracked separately where applicable.

The key comparison isn't simply whether Cadence declines after I sell.

If Cadence rises 15% but my replacement investments rise 25%, the sale may still have added value.

If Cadence rises 30% while my proceeds earn 3% in cash, the decision may have been costly even though I locked in a profit.

This is how I intend to distinguish stock selection from exit timing and capital allocation.

## 12. What would change my mind

I would reconsider Cadence if the valuation became significantly more attractive without a comparable deterioration in the business.

I'd also reconsider it if the company demonstrated that AI-driven design automation was creating substantially more profitable revenue than my current model assumes.

For Synopsys, I'd consider increasing the position if Ansys integration translated into strong cash flow per share, improved returns on invested capital and a clearer path to sustainable margin expansion.

On the other hand, I would consider reducing it further if integration costs, dilution or weaker pricing power materially damaged the expected returns.

A falling share price alone isn't enough to justify a change.

I want to respond to changes in the underlying economics and the relationship between price and expected value, not simply to market movements.

## 13. Final reasoning

I still believe Cadence and Synopsys are among the strongest companies in semiconductor design software.

What I don't believe is that owning both at their current valuations necessarily gives me an attractive enough investment opportunity.

Cadence appears to have too much future success reflected in its price.

Synopsys offers a somewhat better relationship between valuation and expected growth, but the advantage is too uncertain to justify the previous allocation.

I'm therefore selling Cadence completely and keeping a smaller Synopsys position.

The released capital will remain available until I identify a better opportunity. If none appears, I will have to reconsider whether holding cash is preferable to broader market exposure.

Most importantly, I want this decision to remain testable.

In five years, I don't want to look back and come up with a convincing explanation for whatever happened.

I want to know whether my original expectations were accurate, whether my risk assessments were useful, whether I sold too early and whether reallocating the capital actually improved my returns.

I might eventually discover that my ability to select stocks is better than my ability to exit them.

I might discover that my best results come from a small number of high-conviction investments.

Or I might find that actively rotating capital adds no value at all.

Any of those outcomes would be useful, provided I have recorded my expectations before seeing the results.

**The purpose isn't just to make money from this decision. It's to become better at making the next one.**
