# Investing Through the AI Transition

**2026-08-20**

For me, an investment analysis usually does not begin with a stock. It begins with a change. Something changes in technology, economics, regulation, geopolitics, or in the behavior of companies and consumers. The obvious question is who benefits. But that is usually too shallow. The more interesting question is what that change does to the structure of the system. When something suddenly becomes cheaper, faster, or close to abundant, scarcity does not disappear. It moves. That shift is often more interesting than the original innovation itself.

AI is a good example. If intelligence, software development, analysis, and certain forms of labor become much cheaper, that does not mean everything becomes abundant. Quite the opposite. Value shifts toward things that cannot scale at the same speed. Energy becomes more important. Grid connections become more important. Compute capacity becomes more important. Real locations, permits, distribution, customer access, proprietary data, reliable state, permissions, and feedback from the real world can all become relatively more valuable. The easier it becomes to generate an analysis or an interface, the less exceptional the analysis itself becomes. What matters more is whether the underlying data is true, what the actual state of the world is, who can access which systems, and whether a decision can later be connected to its real outcome.

That is a general principle. Abundance does not eliminate scarcity; it relocates it. Once an input becomes abundant, the next question should not only be who produces that input, but which other inputs become bottlenecks because of it. That is where second-order effects begin. More AI does not only mean more demand for GPUs. More compute creates more power demand. More power demand increases pressure on grid connections. Grid constraints increase the value of certain locations. Those locations then require transformers, switchgear, cooling, construction capacity, permits, and other infrastructure. You can start with a technological shift and end up several layers later at a company that barely looks like an AI company at all.

That is why bottlenecks matter. A system can have many excellent inputs and still be limited by one slow or scarce component. That component often determines not only the maximum speed of the system, but also where a disproportionate share of economic value accumulates. If everyone can build models, but only a few companies can reliably access certain data or real workflows, power shifts toward those access points. If enough compute can theoretically be built but a power connection takes seven years, compute is no longer the real constraint. Time-to-power is. The important question is often not which industry is growing, but which necessary part of that growth is actually limiting the system.

There is another related principle: systems should be analyzed from their purpose, not only from their current form. Rules, institutions, products, and markets usually exist because they solve some underlying problem. Technology can change while the purpose remains. That means the current implementation can become obsolete even if the need itself survives. Instead of asking whether a particular software category will grow, it can be more useful to ask what problem that category solves and whether AI can solve the same problem through a completely different structure. This makes the analysis less dependent on how the world happens to be organized today.

Once an interesting scarcity or structural bottleneck becomes visible, the company search begins. But it is important not to stop at the first company that seems to fit. A weak process would be: the grid becomes more important, company X sells grid equipment, therefore company X is the investment. That is not enough. Once the thesis exists, the entire field needs to be mapped. Which companies control the bottleneck? Which only touch one small part of it? Who can expand capacity? Who has the best cost position? Who owns an installed base? Who has distribution? Who has regulatory approvals? Who has customer relationships? Who has access to capital? And who can stop competition from competing away the excess profits?

Company selection is therefore relative. It is not enough that a company is good. It must express the thesis better than the alternatives. Every serious candidate should be compared against at least one real competitor. Why this company and not the next-best one? If there is no strong answer, the selection is probably not deep enough.

Competition itself also has to be defined properly. A company is not automatically a meaningful competitor just because it produces a similar product somewhere in the world. What matters economically is whether customers can actually substitute toward it. A Chinese company may be technologically superior and cheaper, but if American customers cannot realistically use it because of export controls, cybersecurity requirements, political risk, procurement rules, or regulation, then it is only partially a competitor. The same logic works in the other direction. Global competition and accessible competition are not the same thing.

This becomes especially important as globalization weakens. In a fully globalized world, the most efficient supplier can theoretically compete almost everywhere. In a more fragmented world, regional markets, technological blocs, security requirements, different standards, and political barriers emerge. The number of firms that can realistically serve a market may shrink. A company can therefore gain a stronger moat without improving its product at all. It becomes stronger because the world around it becomes less open.

That is why it makes little sense to invest dogmatically only in the largest American companies or only in global market leaders. You are not investing in nationalities. You are investing in competitive structures. A smaller European, Japanese, Israeli, or regional company can be more attractive if it faces less accessible competition, has higher switching costs, or benefits from regulation and infrastructure that protect its market. The relevant question is not only global TAM. It is accessible TAM, and which companies can actually serve it.

The same logic applies inside large corporations. A company may own a small business unit that contributes little to current revenue but represents a large strategic option. These kinds of strategic orphan options are especially interesting. An asset can be nearly invisible in the current P&L and still become extremely valuable if the world moves in its direction. The key is whether management is actually treating that option seriously. Capital allocation often reveals more than rhetoric. If a company begins allocating more capital to a small segment, buying complementary businesses, building infrastructure around it, or integrating it more deeply into the organization, that is often a stronger signal than anything said at a conference.

This leads to another principle: corporate actions can contain information about the future before that future appears in reported revenue. A company that aggressively expands capacity may either be irrationally overinvesting or it may be seeing demand that is not yet fully visible from the outside. Those are completely different situations. CapEx is not inherently bullish or bearish. Its meaning depends on the causal structure behind it. CapEx creates capacity. Capacity must be utilized. Utilization must produce revenue. Revenue must eventually produce margins and cash flow. If that chain is plausible and demand already exists, a large investment program may be the opposite of waste. It may be the cost of removing an existing bottleneck.

That is also why “capacity constrained” can be a very important phrase. If a company lacks capacity while demand already exists, additional CapEx can unlock revenue. That is fundamentally different from a company building capacity first and then hoping customers arrive. The same CapEx number can mean two completely different things at two different companies. Numbers become useful only when they are connected to mechanisms.

Up to this point, you can identify an excellent company and still not have an excellent investment. This separation is critical. A good company and a good stock are not the same thing. The market can price a great company so aggressively that even a fantastic future produces mediocre returns. So after analyzing the company, you have to analyze expectations.

The current price is not a neutral number. It represents expectations. Growth, margins, capital returns, competition, risk, and future cash flows are embedded in it in some form. The central question therefore becomes not only what I believe about the company, but what approximately has to be true for the current price to make sense.

This is where reverse valuation becomes useful. Instead of immediately calculating a price target, the price can be read backward. What growth rate is required? What margin structure is implied? How long must growth persist? What return on capital has to be achieved? If the current valuation only works if the company grows at exceptional rates for a decade, the market already expects a great deal. If the valuation implies a mediocre future while there are strong reasons to expect something much better, the setup becomes more interesting.

Vision and mathematics therefore serve different purposes. Vision helps identify a future that may not yet be obvious. Mathematics prevents a good future from being confused with a cheap stock. You can be completely right that an industry will explode and still make a bad investment if the market already expects that explosion. Every vision eventually has to collide with the price.

The most interesting situations often occur where expectations are uncertain. Uncertainty is not only risk. Uncertainty is opportunity. If everything about a company is already clear and well understood, there is less room for investors to assign radically different probabilities. But where a central question remains unresolved, people can observe the same facts and still assign very different probabilities. That is where mispricing can emerge.

The next question is therefore: what is preventing a higher valuation? Perhaps the market doubts that AI CapEx will earn sufficient returns. Perhaps it believes demand is cyclical. Perhaps it expects stronger competition. Perhaps it sees a new business segment as irrelevant. Perhaps it thinks high costs are structural. Perhaps it believes a regulatory problem is more dangerous than it really is. The central uncertainty has to be identified before you can even know whether you truly disagree with the market.

Then comes the harder question: why should the market be wrong and I be right? Simply disagreeing is not an edge. There has to be a reason for the disagreement. Sometimes that may come from different information, but for a private investor that is unlikely to be the most reliable source of advantage. Different interpretation and different probability models are more interesting. Everyone may see the same numbers, but not everyone gives the same variables the same weight. One investor sees high investment and thinks lower free cash flow. Another sees the same investment, combines it with capacity constraints, backlog, and demand, and interprets it as the prerequisite for future growth.

Mispricing can therefore be understood as a difference between future distributions. The market implicitly assigns probabilities to different outcomes. I may assign different probabilities to those same outcomes. The important difference is not simply my price target minus the current stock price. It is why I may assign a positive scenario a 60 percent probability while the current price appears to reflect something closer to 30 percent.

It is also important to understand why a mispricing could exist in the first place. “Wall Street missed it” is not an explanation. Markets contain many intelligent participants. If I believe I have found a large discrepancy, I should also understand why that discrepancy can persist. Maybe institutional investors have different constraints. They may have to follow benchmarks, maintain liquidity, respect risk limits, or avoid waiting five years for a highly uncertain thesis to resolve. Maybe the company is too small for large funds. Maybe the story is too complex. Maybe the economic payoff sits beyond normal forecasting horizons. Maybe the stock is politically or regulatorily difficult to own. Or maybe the uncertainty is genuinely real and the market has good reasons for demanding a discount. The reason something is not priced in is itself part of the thesis.

That also means being contrarian has no value by itself. A view is not good simply because it differs from consensus. Consensus can be right. The advantage only appears if the causal structure or the probabilities are understood better. Unconventionality without mechanism is not an edge.

This is why every large narrative has to eventually break down into smaller, empirically testable pieces. If I believe AI CapEx will generate enormous returns, that sentence is not enough. I need to explain how. CapEx creates capacity. Capacity becomes available. Customers use it. Usage is monetized. Revenue rises. Margins develop in a particular way. Cash flow follows. Every arrow can fail. Capacity may be built while demand disappoints. Usage may rise while pricing falls faster. Revenue may increase while depreciation and energy costs destroy returns. Only when the chain is explicit do you know what you are actually betting on.

This kind of thinking also helps with second-order effects. A development does not stop at the first visible outcome. If AI demand drives datacenter construction, that can create power scarcity. Power scarcity changes the value of certain locations. Those locations require infrastructure. Infrastructure requires components and permits. New winners appear several steps away from the original technological shift. Instead of stopping at the first obvious beneficiary, the entire causal tree should be followed to identify where scarcity is strongest and competition weakest.

At the same time, every thesis needs a kill condition. Before the investment, there should already be an observation that would seriously damage the theory. If my thesis is that additional capacity will monetize a demand surplus, then rising capacity combined with weak utilization is a serious problem. If the thesis depends on a structural bottleneck and that bottleneck suddenly disappears, the valuation has to change. Without predefined failure conditions, every negative development can be reclassified as temporary, allowing a broken theory to survive indefinitely.

Then comes timing. Timing is a separate problem and cannot simply be inferred from causality. The fact that A eventually causes B does not tell you that B happens next year. A correct future can still be a bad investment if it arrives much later than expected. Timing should therefore begin with conditions, not dates. What has to happen before the final outcome becomes possible? Which of those conditions have already been met? Which are still missing? Which necessary step takes the longest?

If rising AI demand is supposed to create more grid investment, there may be several steps between the initial demand and revenue for a grid company: projects have to be planned, financed, approved, ordered, manufactured, and installed. Each step has its own duration. The slowest required step often sets the lower bound for how quickly the final result can appear. The more physical, regulatory, and organizational the chain is, the wider the timing estimate should be. Software can change in months. Grids, factories, and major industrial systems operate on different time scales.

That is why time windows are more useful than single dates. Instead of saying something happens in the second quarter of 2028, it is better to assign probabilities across periods. Maybe there is a low probability within twelve months, a high probability within two to three years, and a smaller remaining probability after that. Those probabilities can be updated as milestones are reached. Once one required step is completed, the conditional probability of the next step rises. Timing then becomes closer to a hazard-rate problem than a fixed calendar prediction.

Leading indicators matter for the same reason. If you wait until the final outcome appears, the market may already have moved. Revenue is often the endpoint of a longer chain. Orders, backlog, capacity additions, customer contracts, utilization, production data, and capital allocation decisions may appear earlier. If those earlier states are understood, it may be possible to see a thesis progressing before it is fully visible in earnings.

There are also two clocks in markets. The first is the fundamental clock: when does the economic value actually appear? The second is the market clock: when does the market become convinced that the value will appear? Those two moments can be years apart. A company may not generate enormous cash flows for several years, while the stock rerates today because enough evidence has accumulated that those cash flows are likely.

That is why a catalyst is not simply any event. Earnings, product launches, or regulatory decisions only matter if they resolve an important uncertainty. If the central question is whether demand exists, orders or backlog may matter more than EPS. If the central question is whether CapEx is productive, utilization, revenue growth, and margin development may matter more. The best catalyst is the information that tests exactly the point where my probability and the market’s probability diverge.

This creates a particularly interesting structure: large uncertainty, large disagreement, and relatively near resolution. A stock can remain mispriced for years if nothing forces the market to change its mind. But if data will arrive within months that directly answers the central question, repricing can happen very quickly. Mispricing alone is not enough. Resolution speed also matters.

Only after all of this qualitative work should mathematics harden the thesis. I do not want to model one future. I want multiple futures. There is a bear case, a base case, a bull case, and potentially additional states in between. Each scenario has economic assumptions and a probability. This creates an expected value. But the exact number is less important than understanding which assumptions drive the result. If a small change in one variable destroys the entire investment case, the thesis is more fragile than a clean-looking price target suggests.

This also makes comparison with the market more useful. Suppose a stock trades at 100. In the bear case it may be worth 60, in the base case 130, and in the bull case 220. The interesting part is not that 220 is possible. The important question is what probability belongs to each state and what distribution the current price appears to imply. If my distribution is only slightly more optimistic than the market’s, there is little edge. If the distributions differ significantly and I can explain why, the opportunity becomes much more interesting.

Even then, position size should not depend only on how exciting the story is. Sizing should reflect uncertainty, downside, correlation with other positions, timing, confidence, and opportunity cost. A large theoretical mispricing with a fragile causal chain should be treated differently from a smaller mispricing with much higher confidence. Capital committed to one thesis is capital that cannot be used elsewhere.

Finally, the outcome itself is not enough to prove that the analysis was good. A stock can rise for reasons unrelated to the thesis. It can fall even though the underlying analysis was correct and the market simply takes longer to recognize it. Every position should therefore be reviewed after the fact. Was the world model right? Was the bottleneck correctly identified? Was the company selection better than the alternatives? Was competition understood correctly? Were market expectations interpreted correctly? Was the causal chain right? Was the timing good? Did the expected catalyst actually resolve the uncertainty? Or was the result mostly luck?

That is how investing can improve over time. Not by simply counting winners and losers, but by identifying which part of the process is systematically strong or weak. Maybe structural changes are recognized early but monetization is consistently expected too soon. Maybe good companies are found, but competition is not mapped deeply enough. Maybe company analysis is strong but valuation assumptions are too generous. Those mistakes are more useful than a simple return number because they reveal which part of the model needs work.

At the highest level, the whole process is one connected idea. Changes do not create a simple list of winners and losers. They alter the scarcity structure of a system. New scarcities determine where economic power can accumulate. Companies compete to control those scarcities, but not every theoretical competitor is actually accessible in the real world. Globalization, regulation, infrastructure, standards, and geopolitics can redraw competitive boundaries and create winners that would look less attractive in a purely global analysis.

Even the best winner is only interesting if its future is not already fully reflected in the price. That means understanding what the market already expects and where uncertainty remains. That uncertainty is not merely a problem; it is the space in which mispricing can exist. The task is to structure that uncertainty better, understand the mechanism behind it, estimate when it will become visible, identify what evidence could resolve it, and finally test whether the gap between market expectations and the most likely future is large enough to justify the bet.

A good investment is therefore not simply a correct prediction about the future. It requires seeing a future, understanding the economic structure behind it, selecting the right beneficiary, separating real competition from theoretical competition, knowing what expectations are already embedded in the price, identifying a meaningful uncertainty that can be assessed better than the market, understanding how and when that uncertainty will resolve, and ensuring that the potential payoff is large enough relative to the remaining uncertainty.

That is where vision, company analysis, competition, probabilities, timing, and valuation become one system. Vision tells you where the world may be moving. Bottlenecks show where scarcity is created. Competition determines who can monetize that scarcity. Market analysis reveals what is already expected. Uncertainty creates room for mispricing. Causality shows whether the thesis can actually work. Timing shows when it becomes relevant. Catalysts show when the market may be forced to update. Mathematics shows whether the difference is large enough. And the later review tells you whether the result came from skill or luck.
