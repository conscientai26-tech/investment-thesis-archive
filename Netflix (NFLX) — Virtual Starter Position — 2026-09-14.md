# Netflix (NFLX) — Virtual Starter Position — 2026-09-14

**Position:** $6,000 virtual  
**Reference price:** $77.40  
**Implied shares:** ~77.52  
**Portfolio:** $1,000,000 reference portfolio

## Why I bought it

I am starting Netflix with $6,000.

The size is not small because I think the thesis is weak. It is small because this is a newer field for me. I understand AI infrastructure, enterprise software, cloud, bottlenecks and capital conversion much better than I understand film economics, franchise formation and long-term entertainment behavior.

That matters. I do not want to confuse confidence in the company with confidence in my own model of the industry.

Netflix is also useful for another reason: I want to know whether the five investment theses I have been using are actually general principles or whether they just happen to work well during the AI cycle. Entertainment is very different from semiconductors, datacenters or enterprise software. It is creative, probabilistic and slow. If the same framework still works here, that is much stronger evidence that the framework itself is useful.

So the $6,000 position is doing two things at once. It is an investment in Netflix, and it is a live test of my framework outside the area where I developed it.

## The actual thesis

The basic idea is not that Netflix fell 40% and therefore has to rebound.

What interests me is that Netflix may look mature for the wrong reason.

Streaming is mature. Netflix as an entertainment company may not be.

Netflix already has the difficult part that many media companies spend decades trying to build: global distribution, hundreds of millions of accounts, a direct payment relationship, recommendation, a huge amount of viewing data, a global production network and a brand people already open when they want entertainment.

What it does not yet have at the same level as Disney or Warner is decades of accumulated premium IP and a fully developed system for turning hits into franchises that can be monetized again and again.

That difference is important. Netflix is old as a company, but Netflix as a global original-content studio is young compared with the old Hollywood studios.

The question is whether its thinner franchise library is a structural weakness or simply the result of having had much less time.

I lean toward the second explanation being at least partly true. Netflix has already shown that it can create or globalize major hits repeatedly. Stranger Things, Squid Game and Bridgerton are enough to show that this is not a one-hit accident. What is less proven is whether Netflix can turn that ability into a large stock of long-lived IP that compounds for decades.

That is where most of the upside sits for me.

A normal hit creates viewing hours. A real franchise can create sequels, spin-offs, games, ads, merchandise, live experiences and library value for years. If Netflix becomes better at that conversion, the economics of one successful title become much more valuable without Netflix needing to reinvent its distribution each time.

## Why Netflix specifically

The film industry has a hard bottleneck: you cannot order cultural relevance with capital.

Netflix can spend more money, hire more people and use better tools, but none of that guarantees the next Harry Potter. This is one reason I am not sizing the position aggressively. The creative bottleneck is real.

But Netflix is one of the few companies where I can see a plausible way to improve the odds over time.

It owns the full feedback loop:

**create → distribute globally → observe what people actually watch → learn → recommend better → fund the next projects.**

AI can make that loop faster and cheaper without needing to replace creativity itself. Better post-production, localization, visual effects, search, recommendation, marketing and advertising all matter. Netflix has already started using AI in production workflows and has bought InterPositive, which is a much stronger signal than simply talking about AI on an earnings call.

The interesting moat is therefore not “Netflix has better AI than Google.” It obviously does not.

The possible moat is that Netflix can apply AI inside a closed entertainment system that it already controls. The same technology can improve production, discovery and monetization at once.

If AI makes content cheaper to create, that may even increase the value of Netflix's existing core. More content does not create more human time. If supply explodes while attention stays fixed, discovery, distribution, trusted brands and strong IP become more important.

That is the bottleneck-migration part of the thesis.

The risk is equally clear: the bottleneck can migrate in the right direction and Netflix can still lose. YouTube, creator platforms or future AI-native entertainment could capture that scarce attention instead.

## How the five theses fit

**Scarce-Core Expansion:** Netflix's scarce core is not any one show. It is global distribution, recommendation, the account/payment relationship, brand and the feedback generated by a massive audience. If entertainment supply becomes more abundant, those things may become more valuable.

**Catch-up Giant:** this is probably the strongest fit. Netflix does not need to catch up in streaming. It can still catch up in owned IP, franchise conversion, advertising, live, games and physical experiences. That is attractive because the company doing the catching up already owns the distribution layer.

**Bottleneck Asymmetry:** weaker fit, but still present. Advertising is the clearest example. Netflix did not need to acquire a new audience before monetizing it with ads. The audience was already there, so a relatively small additional layer can produce a lot of value.

**Deep Bottleneck:** creating durable cultural IP is genuinely difficult. Money helps, but it does not solve the problem deterministically. Netflix does not have an ASML-style monopoly, but the combination of production, distribution, data, recommendation and capital is hard to recreate quickly.

**Bottleneck Migration:** probably the most important long-term idea. If AI and creator tools make content abundant, scarcity can move toward attention, discovery, distribution and recognizable IP. Netflix is already strong in several of those layers.

The framework therefore transfers surprisingly well to a business that has almost nothing to do with datacenters or enterprise software. That is part of why I want the position.

## The Warner attempt matters

The failed Warner Bros. deal changed how I think about Netflix.

It showed that management is willing to think much bigger than “keep making more Netflix shows.” Warner would have given Netflix decades of accumulated IP, studio infrastructure, HBO, talent relationships and a huge library immediately.

That would have been a shortcut through a problem that otherwise takes decades.

Netflix did not lose Warner because it was incapable of financing a large deal. Paramount offered more and Netflix chose not to keep bidding. I like that combination: they were willing to attempt something transformative, but they still had a price ceiling.

That tells me two things. First, management probably sees ownership of premium content assets as strategically important. Second, it does not appear willing to destroy capital just to win an auction.

The thesis does not require another Warner-sized acquisition. Organic creation, smaller acquisitions, licensing and partnerships can all move Netflix in the same direction more slowly.

## What the market already knows

This is where I have to be careful.

The market is not pricing Netflix like a broken company. Consensus already expects strong revenue growth, large margin expansion and high earnings growth through 2030.

So my edge cannot simply be “Netflix will grow more than Wall Street thinks.” That is too easy and probably wrong.

The more interesting disagreement is duration.

The market can be broadly right about Netflix through 2030 and still be too pessimistic about what Netflix looks like after 2030.

If streaming is only the mature distribution core and Netflix is still early in building an IP/franchise/ads/live/games ecosystem on top of it, then the period of high returns on capital may last longer than a normal mature media-company valuation assumes.

That is the real variant perception.

## The math I care about

At $77.40, I do not need Netflix to return to the extreme valuation it had at the 2025 peak.

Using roughly the current 2030 consensus EPS of $6.46, Netflix only needs a high-teens earnings multiple around 2030 to produce something close to a reasonable S&P 500 return from here. At around 20x earnings, the return becomes clearly more attractive.

That matters because it gives the thesis some room to be imperfect.

I do not need all of these things to work at once:

- perfect franchise creation,
- huge games success,
- huge live success,
- AI transforming film production,
- another giant acquisition,
- a return to a 30–50x multiple.

The existing business can do a lot of the work. The ecosystem thesis is upside on top of an already profitable machine.

I also have to normalize 2026 because the Warner termination fee boosted reported earnings and free cash flow. I do not want to call Netflix cheaper than it really is by treating a one-time payment as recurring profit.

## Why the buybacks matter

Netflix is already producing enough cash to keep investing and still repurchase large amounts of stock.

That is important after a large valuation reset. If the intrinsic value of the company has fallen less than the share price, buybacks become more valuable because Netflix can retire more shares for the same amount of cash.

This is not the thesis by itself. Bad companies can destroy value with buybacks too. But for a business that remains strong, lower prices make the capital return more useful.

## What would make me add

I would be comfortable moving from $6,000 toward $8,000–$10,000 if I see evidence that my understanding of the industry is improving and Netflix is validating the second-stage thesis.

The things I care about most are simple: Netflix keeps producing major hits; more of those hits become durable franchises; advertising keeps scaling without damaging the product; AI actually improves production economics instead of being marketing language; new formats strengthen the core instead of becoming expensive side projects; and content spending grows slower than the economic value Netflix gets from the content.

I also want to see that the market is not already pricing all of this as inevitable before I add.

## What would prove me wrong

One failed movie means almost nothing. This is a probabilistic business.

What would worry me is a pattern: engagement weakening while content spending keeps rising, pricing creating more churn, ads maturing early, games/live/experiences failing to improve the core, Netflix repeatedly creating hits but failing to turn them into durable franchises, or attention shifting structurally toward YouTube, creators or AI-native entertainment.

The deepest failure would be that I correctly identify attention and discovery as the next bottleneck but choose the wrong company to own it.

I would also become more cautious if management starts paying obviously uneconomic prices for large acquisitions just to accelerate the IP strategy.

## Why only $6,000

Because I like the thesis more than I trust my current entertainment model.

That is the whole point of the size.

Netflix may deserve a larger position later. But I want to earn that increase by learning the industry and watching the thesis develop rather than starting with a large position because the story sounds convincing.

The position is meaningful enough to count, small enough that model error is acceptable, and useful as a test of whether my five theses actually work outside the AI-heavy areas where I built them.

So this investment has two questions attached to it:

**Was Netflix a good stock at this price?**

and

**Do my five investment theses still work when the industry is old, creative, slow and probabilistic?**

That second question is almost as interesting to me as the first.
