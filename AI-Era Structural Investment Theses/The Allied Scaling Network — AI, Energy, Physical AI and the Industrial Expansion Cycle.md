# The Allied Scaling Network — AI, Energy, Physical AI and the Industrial Expansion Cycle

**24 August 2026**

This essay records a developing investment framework rather than a finished prediction. The central idea began with a question that is easy to state but much harder to model correctly: what happens when AI stops being primarily a software story and begins changing the rate at which entire economies can discover, finance, build and deploy new productive capacity?

The usual way of discussing AI is to start with models, chips, datacenters and software companies. That is useful, but incomplete. If intelligence becomes cheaper, the economic consequence is not simply that existing tasks cost less. Cheaper intelligence can increase the number of things worth attempting. More designs can be generated, more software can be built, more industrial processes can be optimized, more experiments can be run, more products can be tested, and more physical systems can eventually be automated. If that happens, scarcity does not disappear. It migrates.

That is the broader principle behind this research: **when one important input becomes abundant, value tends to move toward the inputs that cannot expand at the same speed.** AI may make reasoning, software creation, analysis and some forms of coordination much cheaper. It cannot make a high-voltage transformer appear instantly. It cannot eliminate a seven-year grid connection queue by generating more tokens. It cannot immediately create an advanced semiconductor fab, a nuclear fuel chain, a deep pool of industrial technicians, a precision robotics cluster, or trusted access to strategic materials. The faster the digital layer moves, the more visible these slower layers can become.

This is where the investment problem begins. The objective is not to find companies that can put the word AI in a presentation. It is to follow the causal chain until the system collides with something genuinely scarce, then determine who owns that scarcity, whether they can capture economic rents from it, and whether the market already expects the outcome.

The Allied Scaling Network thesis is one extension of that process. It asks whether the United States may possess a much larger effective resource base than its domestic economy alone suggests, because it sits at the center of an unusually powerful network of technologically advanced allies. If the productive capabilities of those allies become even partially more combinable, the resulting system could behave less like a set of independent economies and more like a distributed production network. AI and geopolitical competition may increase the pressure to make that transition.

This is not a prediction that NATO becomes a country. It is not a prediction that sovereign democracies stop pursuing national interests. It is a framework for identifying where coordination, specialization, redundancy and physical capacity could alter the economics of strategic industries — and where public markets may misprice the resulting changes.

---

## 1. The hidden resource base of the United States

The United States already has an extraordinary domestic resource base. It has frontier AI research, hyperscale cloud infrastructure, deep capital markets, world-leading universities, chip-design capability, military power, software distribution, large energy resources, extensive land, and some of the most productive companies ever built.

But there is another asset that is normally treated as external to the American economic balance sheet: **the productive capacity of its allies.**

Japan contains extraordinary capabilities in robotics, precision manufacturing, machine tools, industrial engineering, components and advanced materials. South Korea is deeply important in memory semiconductors, electronics, batteries, shipbuilding and high-volume manufacturing. The Netherlands controls unusually scarce semiconductor-equipment capability. Israel has a dense cybersecurity, defense-tech and adversarial-deployment ecosystem. France retains deep expertise in nuclear technology, aerospace and defense. Germany remains strong in machinery, automation, chemicals and enterprise industrial systems. Canada and Australia possess large reserves of minerals, energy and land. The United Kingdom contributes finance, aerospace, defense, research and a close security relationship with the United States.

The United States does not need to recreate all of these capabilities from zero. In principle, it can access many of them through a trusted network.

The problem is that these capabilities still exist largely as separate national systems.

The United States has an alliance network. It does not yet fully possess an **alliance production system**.

That distinction may become much more important in an era in which AI, robotics, semiconductors, energy, defense production and advanced manufacturing increasingly overlap. If technological competition becomes a race not only to invent but to deploy at enormous scale, the decisive question may no longer be which country has the single best technology. It may be which political system can mobilize the largest amount of trusted capability quickly enough to remove the next bottleneck.

---

## 2. From alliances to a distributed production system

Traditional alliances are usually described through military guarantees, intelligence sharing, diplomatic coordination and weapons procurement. Those remain important, but the boundary between economic capacity and national-security capacity is becoming less clear.

A semiconductor fab is commercial infrastructure until access to advanced chips becomes a strategic constraint. A datacenter is commercial infrastructure until compute becomes a military and scientific capability. A grid connection is an ordinary utility problem until the inability to power new factories or AI clusters becomes a national bottleneck. Robotics is industrial automation until the ability to scale physical production becomes part of geopolitical competition.

The relevant question therefore changes from simply asking, “Who would fight with the United States?” to asking:

> **Which productive capabilities exist inside the trusted American network, and how quickly can they be combined when a new strategic bottleneck appears?**

For analytical purposes, this allows the allied system to be treated as something like a **distributed synthetic economy**. The countries remain sovereign. They keep their own governments, tax systems, industrial policies and political interests. But we can still ask what the combined system would be capable of if barriers between trusted members became less restrictive in selected strategic industries.

The answer matters because capabilities are not merely additive. They can multiply each other.

American frontier models become more valuable when Japanese and Korean companies can embody them in machines, sensors and industrial systems. Japanese robotics becomes more valuable when paired with American models, cloud, chips and capital. Australian minerals become more strategically useful when they feed Korean, Japanese, European and American manufacturing. Israeli cybersecurity becomes more valuable as billions of autonomous AI actions create a larger attack surface. American capital becomes more productive when it can finance the strongest capability anywhere inside the trusted network rather than only domestically.

The resulting loop can look like this:

**specialization → larger market → greater scale → lower unit costs → more deployment → more learning → better capability → deeper specialization.**

A network that reaches sufficient integration can therefore generate increasing returns of its own.

## 3. The Coordination Discount

The easiest way to model the unrealized value of this network is through what I call the **Coordination Discount**.

Suppose the allied world contains 100 units of theoretical technological and industrial capability. That does not mean all 100 units can actually be mobilized for a common economic or strategic objective. Between them sit export controls, separate procurement systems, incompatible standards, security classifications, national subsidies, domestic-content rules, IP restrictions, certification regimes, regulatory differences, national champions, political mistrust and ordinary bureaucracy.

Perhaps only 60 of the theoretical 100 units are effectively combinable.

One way to increase Western productive capacity is to invent more technology and build more factories. Another is simply to make existing capability easier to combine. If standards, procurement, capital flows, manufacturing access and technology-sharing improve enough to move effective utilization from 60 to 75, the system has increased its usable capacity by 25 percent without discovering a new scientific principle.

The resources were already there. Institutional friction prevented them from becoming fully economic.

This is structurally similar to the American domestic capacity problem. The United States can have abundant land while zoning prevents it from becoming housing. It can have abundant energy resources while transmission and interconnection queues prevent them from becoming usable electricity. It can have capital and technology while permitting delays prevent them from becoming factories.

At the alliance level, the same problem reappears:

> **Capability exists, but institutional fragmentation prevents it from becoming combined capacity.**

The Coordination Discount is therefore another kind of conversion layer.

---

## 4. Why the Coordination Discount will never disappear

The strongest criticism of this framework is also one of the most important parts of it: sovereign democracies are not subsidiaries of a holding company.

A US senator has political incentives to secure a factory in Ohio rather than in Bavaria or Kyushu. France wants French strategic capacity. European governments care about technological sovereignty. The United States worries about dependence on foreign supply chains. Governments protect national champions. Election cycles change priorities. Security requirements differ. Industrial policy is often designed to capture domestic jobs, tax revenue and political credit rather than maximize the theoretical efficiency of an alliance-wide production function.

This means the Coordination Discount is not simply a technical bug waiting to be removed. Part of it is a political feature.

A more realistic decomposition is:

**Coordination Discount = technical friction + regulatory friction + security friction + bureaucratic friction + sovereign political interest.**

The first four components can fall substantially. The final component probably never disappears.

This changes the thesis in an important way. The prediction is not that thirty democracies eventually agree on a perfectly rational industrial division of labor. The more plausible outcome is selective integration in areas where the strategic cost of fragmentation becomes larger than the domestic political benefit of independence.

That can happen sector by sector, treaty by treaty and crisis by crisis.

This is why bilateral and small multilateral relationships may matter more than a single grand alliance plan. The US-Japan relationship can deepen around semiconductors, robotics or advanced manufacturing without requiring France to agree. The US-Israel relationship can deepen around AI security and defense technology without requiring a universal NATO industrial constitution. AUKUS can reduce defense-trade barriers among the United States, Australia and the United Kingdom without waiting for every allied government to harmonize its rules.

The likely architecture is therefore modular rather than universal.

## 5. The Shadow Price of Time

Political preferences are not fixed. Their relative importance changes when a bottleneck becomes urgent.

In normal conditions, a government may prefer domestic production even if a trusted ally can produce the same capability more efficiently. But suppose a technological threshold is crossed and the capability suddenly becomes strategically critical. If reproducing it domestically requires five years while an ally already has scalable capacity, the cost of waiting rises sharply.

This creates what can be thought of as the **Shadow Price of Time**.

The more valuable speed becomes, the more expensive fragmentation becomes.

A major Physical AI breakthrough could create this effect. So could a semiconductor shortage, rapid Chinese advances in autonomous manufacturing, a war, an energy shortage, or a step-change in autonomous defense systems. Once the cost of delay becomes high enough, governments can accept forms of allied specialization that would have been politically unattractive in normal conditions.

This creates the possibility of nonlinear integration. For years, the system may look fragmented and bureaucratic. Then a strategic threshold raises the price of delay and several barriers fall at once in one sector.

From an investment perspective, that means the important signal is not vague diplomatic language. It is a change in economic behavior: cross-border procurement, technology-sharing, joint production, common standards, export-control exemptions, guaranteed orders and private capital being directed into allied production capacity.

## 6. Early evidence that the architecture is becoming more concrete

The stronger version of the thesis remains speculative, but the direction is no longer hypothetical.

At the 2026 NATO Summit, NATO launched an Innovation Scale-Up Package built around three unusually economic ideas: **asserting Allied demand, mobilizing private capital and facilitating manufacturing**. NATO explicitly states that non-traditional suppliers need credible adoption pathways in order to scale and that clearer demand signals, joint procurement, risk sharing and public-private financing can increase private investment in defense production. The package also introduces the NATO Engine, a network intended to connect companies that need production capacity with factories across the Alliance that have flexible capacity available. NATO says the model should encourage cross-border collaboration and allow companies from across the Alliance to identify production partners. The NSPA pilot already covers all 32 allies, more than 840 companies and more than 100 active opportunities. [NATO Innovation Scale-Up Package](https://www.nato.int/en/about-us/official-texts-and-resources/official-texts/2026/07/08/nato-innovation-scale-up-package) · [NSPA NATO Engine](https://www.nspa.nato.int/news/2026/nspa-launches-nato-engine-platform-to-expand-alliance-production-capacity)

This is not a distributed state. It is, however, a concrete attempt to reduce coordination friction between demand, capital and manufacturing capacity.

AUKUS provides another useful prototype. The United States, Australia and the United Kingdom have spent years reducing defense-trade barriers and building reciprocal exemptions designed to make most defense trade between authorized users substantially easier. The US Department of Defense described these changes as creating conditions for deeper integration of the three industrial bases. A final US rule published at the end of 2025 further refined the exemption regime. [AUKUS Defense Ministers’ Joint Statement](https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3733790/aukus-defense-ministers-joint-statement/) · [US defense-trade exemption overview](https://2021-2025.state.gov/key-elements-of-the-international-traffic-in-arms-regulations-exemption-for-defense-trade-and-cooperation-among-australia-the-united-kingdom-and-the-united-states/)

Israel offers a different example. In January 2026, Israel and the United States signed a bilateral statement on artificial intelligence after Israel joined Pax Silica. The statement covers cooperation in areas including AI, advanced computing, semiconductors and other strategic technologies. [Israel–US AI Joint Statement](https://embassies.gov.il/nepal/en/node/9937)

Japan is particularly important for the Physical AI portion of the thesis. In June 2026, Japan’s Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry launched a program to develop multimodal foundation models for AI robots and Physical AI. METI explicitly described Japan’s broad industrial base and real-world manufacturing data as a potential competitive advantage and framed Physical AI as a route to maintaining industrial competitiveness. [METI Physical AI program](https://www.meti.go.jp/press/2026/06/20260630005/20260630005.html)

These initiatives remain fragmented. They do not prove that the larger network thesis will be realized. What they show is that the underlying mechanisms — common demand signals, private-capital mobilization, shared manufacturing, export-control reform, strategic AI cooperation and Physical AI industrial policy — already exist in early form.

## 7. Trusted-network globalization

The possible long-term structure is neither the globalization model of the last several decades nor complete autarky.

Traditional globalization often optimized for cost: produce wherever the combination of labor, capital, logistics and regulation is cheapest.

The reaction to geopolitical fragmentation can easily move too far in the opposite direction: produce everything domestically, even when doing so destroys scale and duplicates capabilities that already exist in trusted countries.

A more durable architecture would distinguish between **adversarial dependency** and **trusted-network dependency**.

Dependence on a potential strategic opponent for an irreplaceable military or semiconductor component is a fundamentally different risk from dependence on Japan, Australia, Canada or the United Kingdom. That does not mean allied dependencies are harmless. Governments change, wars happen, supply chains break and even trusted countries can pursue conflicting interests. But the risk is categorically different.

The stronger principle may therefore become:

> **Domestic where sovereignty genuinely requires it; allied specialization where scale, speed and cost matter more.**

This still requires redundancy. If one Dutch company controls an indispensable process in the semiconductor supply chain, the alliance cannot simply declare the problem solved. Critical nodes need inventories, geographically distributed maintenance, replacement components, backup production and enough duplicated knowledge to survive disruption.

The optimal architecture is therefore not maximum specialization. It is **specialization with strategic redundancy**.

## 8. Strategic redundancy: valuable for states, dangerous for shareholders

Strategic redundancy creates one of the most interesting investment traps in the entire framework.

From the perspective of national security, building a second supplier can be rational even if the second supplier is less efficient. A country may willingly pay more for a duplicate semiconductor fab, a domestic uranium-enrichment facility or an alternative defense supplier because the option value of resilience exceeds the efficiency loss.

But what is good for the state is not automatically good for equity holders.

Redundancy can lower asset utilization, increase capital intensity, create overcapacity and reduce industry ROIC. A government may consider a strategically redundant factory successful even if it would never have been built by a purely profit-maximizing private investor.

This leads to one of the most useful investment rules produced by the discussion:

> **Don’t own the redundancy. Own what redundancy duplicates demand for.**

If the United States, Europe and Japan each build more semiconductor capacity for security reasons, the duplicate fabs may compete away their own economics. But they may all still need lithography systems, deposition equipment, inspection systems, power infrastructure, cables, cooling, substations and specialized construction.

Political inefficiency can therefore become a demand multiplier for upstream bottlenecks.

This matters because it gives us a robustness test. The strongest companies may benefit in both worlds:

**If allied coordination succeeds**, specialized industrial capacity expands and they sell more.

**If allied coordination remains messy**, countries duplicate capacity and they may still sell more — sometimes even more per unit of final output.

That is a much stronger investment structure than depending on a single political forecast.

## 9. The new robustness filter

The investment process has therefore become more demanding.

The objective is not to buy what consensus investors call safe. It is to find situations where our own analysis sees relatively few plausible paths to permanent impairment while the market still appears to underestimate the upside.

Two variables matter most:

**Expected mispricing** — how different is our probability distribution from the one implied by the price?

**Thesis robustness** — how many plausible future states still produce an acceptable economic outcome?

The ideal position is not simply a company with enormous upside in one narrow future. It is a company where several independent causal chains converge on the same cash flow.

A particularly attractive structure would look like this:

**AI expands rapidly → demand increases.**

**Physical AI develops → demand increases again.**

**Allied integration succeeds → demand increases.**

**Allied integration fails partially and national duplication increases → demand still increases.**

**Defense and reindustrialization expand → demand increases.**

The more independent pathways lead to the same bottleneck, the less the investment depends on being exactly right about one grand narrative.

This is the logic behind the phrase: **be the grid, not the gadget.**

## 10. Energy is the shared substrate of the entire system

The most obvious investment implication from the broader framework is energy, but the useful version of the thesis is larger than “AI needs electricity.” That observation is already widely understood.

The stronger claim is that AI may trigger a broad industrial-capacity expansion in which electricity becomes the shared scarce input across several simultaneous waves of investment.

AI datacenters need power. Semiconductor fabs need power. Defense factories need power. Robotics manufacturing needs power. Battery and material processing need power. Reshoring needs power. If AI makes a large number of new industrial projects economically viable, those projects need power too.

The demand stack can therefore become:

**AI expansion + datacenter expansion + semiconductor reshoring + defense rearmament + robotics manufacturing + battery and materials processing + industrial reshoring → electrical demand shock.**

Electricity becomes the common substrate of the scaling system.

The important investment question is then not simply how much electricity will be generated. It is where the chain between theoretical generation and usable megawatts breaks.

## 11. Time-to-Power as the real bottleneck

A country can possess abundant gas, uranium, solar, wind or hydro resources and still be unable to connect a new factory in time.

The real chain is closer to:

**generation → transmission → substation → transformer → switchgear → interconnection → permits → construction → physical connection → usable MW.**

If one of those steps requires five years, that step becomes the effective bottleneck regardless of how much theoretical energy exists.

This is why **Time-to-Power** may be more economically important than electricity supply in the abstract.

Potential bottlenecks include transformers, switchgear, high-voltage cables, substations, transmission construction, specialized engineering, skilled labor, cooling, power electronics, permitting and land with immediate grid access.

The investment edge cannot be “the grid will grow.” The market already knows that grids need investment. The edge has to be more specific:

> **Which part of the chain remains scarce for longer than consensus expects, and which company can capture the scarcity without destroying returns through excessive capital spending?**

That distinction is central.

## 12. AI efficiency may increase total energy demand

A common counterargument to the energy thesis is that AI systems will become much more efficient. That is almost certainly true. But efficiency per task does not determine total consumption by itself.

Suppose the energy required per useful AI task falls by half. If the number of economically useful tasks quadruples, total energy consumption doubles.

**0.5 × 4 = 2.**

AI may be unusually susceptible to this effect because cheaper intelligence can be reinvested into more agents, simulations, evaluations, experiments, reasoning, monitoring, R&D and product development. In competitive environments, a company that gets twice as much useful intelligence from the same power budget does not necessarily shut down half its datacenter. It can use the efficiency gain to do more work.

The industrial second order matters even more. Cheaper AI can make more real-world projects viable. Those projects create permanent workloads and physical assets that consume electricity independently of the original AI training or inference task.

The loop becomes:

**cheaper intelligence → more discoveries → more viable projects → more factories and infrastructure → more electricity demand → more generation and grid investment → more capacity → more AI and industrial activity.**

Efficiency reduces energy intensity. It does not guarantee lower absolute energy use.

---

## 13. Energy can reorganize industrial geography

If AI and robotics reduce the relative importance of labor costs, energy can become more important in deciding where industrial activity takes place.

The future industrial location equation may place increasing weight on:

**cost per MWh, time-to-power, reliability, grid expansion speed, land, permitting and trusted jurisdiction.**

A heavily automated factory has less reason to choose a country merely because labor is cheap. It still needs enormous quantities of reliable electricity, physical land, logistics and political stability.

This could create new strategic advantages for regions with abundant power and fast connection times. Parts of Canada, Australia, the Nordic countries, France and selected US states could become more attractive not only for datacenters but for power-intensive manufacturing, materials processing and automated industry.

The location itself becomes an asset.

The feedback loop can then look like:

**energy abundance → industrial attraction → cluster formation → more infrastructure → more investment → deeper cluster.**

That is the same specialization-compounding mechanism at a regional level.

---

## 14. Nuclear and trusted energy capacity

Nuclear power fits this framework because the relevant asset is not only cheap electricity. It is reliable, long-duration, high-capacity-factor electricity with a fuel chain that can be made strategically secure.

If AI and industrial loads remain structurally high for decades, the value of reliable 24/7 power can rise. But the nuclear investment chain must be analyzed more carefully than simply buying uranium producers.

The actual chain includes:

**uranium mining → conversion → enrichment → fuel fabrication → reactor technology → engineering → servicing.**

The critical scarcity may sit anywhere inside that chain.

The relevant geopolitical question is not merely whether enough uranium exists globally. It is how much trusted and politically accessible mining, conversion, enrichment, fuel fabrication and reactor capability exists inside the allied system.

Again:

> **Global supply is not the same as accessible strategic supply.**

This can create opportunities not only in miners but in enrichment, fuel services, nuclear engineering, reactor components and existing nuclear generation.

The same warning about redundancy applies. Governments may subsidize strategically important nuclear capacity even when private returns are mediocre. The best equity may be the scarce supplier whose product every expansion requires rather than the project carrying the largest capital burden.

## 15. The second-order energy trade: new economic territory

The energy buildout can create a second investment cycle that is easy to miss.

Suppose AI datacenters and industrial reshoring force governments and companies to build new generation, transmission and grid infrastructure. That infrastructure does not disappear once the initial projects are complete. It remains available to other industries.

More abundant electricity can improve the economics of metals processing, chemicals, desalination, synthetic fuels, automated manufacturing, mining, transport and entirely new industrial clusters.

The sequence becomes:

**AI drives energy buildout → energy scarcity falls at the margin → previously uneconomic industries become viable → new demand appears → further energy expansion follows.**

AI can therefore indirectly finance the physical infrastructure that enables a broader industrial expansion later.

This is why the long-term electricity thesis may outlive the first datacenter boom.

---

## 16. Physical AI may be the most important accelerator

Physical AI is potentially the point at which several parts of the framework converge.

As long as AI remains primarily software, the United States can dominate a large part of the stack through models, cloud, chips and software platforms. Physical AI requires a much broader industrial base.

Robots require motors, servos, precision reducers, sensors, cameras, power electronics, batteries, controllers, machine tools, factories, materials, safety systems and industrial integration. Many of these capabilities are unusually strong in Japan, South Korea and parts of Europe.

This creates an especially interesting complementarity:

**US frontier intelligence × Japanese physical execution × Korean electronics and memory × European industrial machinery × Australian and Canadian resources.**

If AI crosses the threshold from impressive robotic demonstrations to systems that are reliably cheaper or more productive than human labor across many industrial tasks, the economic question changes quickly. It is no longer “Can the robot perform the task?” It becomes “How many can we manufacture, integrate and power?”

At that point, the bottleneck can move from intelligence to physical embodiment and deployment.

This is why Japan is currently one of the highest-priority countries in the research framework. The country has a deep public-market ecosystem across robotics, machine tools, sensors, components and semiconductor equipment. The key question is not whether Japan is generally good at manufacturing. It is which existing Japanese capabilities become much more scarce when intelligence gets cheaper.

Possible nodes include industrial robots, servo systems, machine vision, precision gearing, linear motion, sensors, motors, factory automation and semiconductor manufacturing equipment.

The most interesting companies may look boring before the bottleneck migrates.

## 17. AI can increase the value of national specialization

Historically, a small country faced natural ceilings even when it was unusually good at one technology. Its labor force and domestic market were limited.

AI and a larger trusted market can weaken those ceilings.

Imagine Israel develops a particularly strong AI-security ecosystem. AI allows a relatively small number of experts to supervise far more automated security activity. American compute and cloud allow those systems to operate at scale. A larger allied market expands demand. Private capital finances larger companies. More deployment produces more adversarial and failure data. That strengthens the local ecosystem further.

The relevant equation becomes:

**national comparative advantage × AI leverage × alliance-scale market.**

The same logic can apply to Japanese engineering, Korean memory, Dutch semiconductor equipment or French nuclear expertise.

The alliance therefore does not merely allocate existing comparative advantages. It can reinforce them through larger demand and deeper clusters.

The loop becomes:

**specialization → allied demand → capital → scale → learning → stronger capability → more allied demand.**

This is **Allied Specialization Compounding**.

## 18. Accessible-TAM expansion may be one of the largest mispricing mechanisms

One of the most attractive investment implications has little to do with discovering a new technology.

A company can already have excellent technology and still be limited by a small accessible market because procurement rules, certifications, national security requirements or political boundaries prevent it from competing broadly.

If those boundaries weaken, the economics of the company can change before the technology changes at all.

An Israeli defense or cybersecurity company serving a small home market is one business. The same company with practical access to a much larger US-led procurement and security ecosystem is a different business. A Japanese industrial specialist that can sell into a much larger Physical AI buildout has a different reinvestment runway. A small NATO-country defense supplier whose products become interoperable across many allied militaries can justify far more manufacturing capacity than its domestic budget ever could.

The mechanism is:

**market-access barriers fall → accessible TAM rises → expected demand becomes more credible → capital investment becomes rational → scale improves → unit costs fall → cluster learning increases → competitive position strengthens.**

This can produce a rerating without a new patent or product breakthrough.

The technology stayed the same. The economic environment changed.

For public markets, this may be one of the cleanest ways the Allied Scaling Network creates alpha: companies may still be valued according to a national or fragmented market even as their realistic accessible market begins to expand toward the trusted bloc.

## 19. Common procurement can change the economics of capacity investment

Strategic manufacturing often suffers from a simple problem: uncertainty about future demand.

A company may have the technology to build a much larger factory but refuse to do so because it cannot justify billions of dollars of capital without enough visibility into orders.

A credible multi-country demand signal changes that calculation.

If several governments collectively demonstrate that they will need a capability for years, the risk of capacity investment falls. Financing becomes easier. Larger plants become rational. Suppliers invest around them. Unit costs fall. Skills accumulate. The local cluster deepens.

The loop becomes:

**allied demand → lower investment uncertainty → private capital → larger capacity → lower unit costs → greater capability → more procurement.**

This is industrial policy at network scale.

NATO’s 2026 Innovation Scale-Up Package is important precisely because it explicitly links Allied demand, private capital and manufacturing capacity. It is an early attempt to solve the missing middle between a government saying that a technology is strategically important and a company being confident enough to build production capacity for it.

The most relevant industries include defense manufacturing, autonomous systems, drones, munitions, semiconductors, robotics, power electronics and strategic materials.

## 20. Standards and interoperability can become economic infrastructure

A more connected network also requires common interfaces.

Shared production, procurement and autonomous systems need identity, security, communications protocols, APIs, certification, hardware interfaces, data models, supply-chain verification and permission systems.

This creates another potential investment layer: **network infrastructure**.

Fragmentation itself creates demand for interoperability. Successful integration increases the scale over which the interoperability layer operates.

That gives some standards a potentially attractive dual property:

**if the system remains fragmented, translation and interoperability are necessary; if the system becomes integrated, the successful standard scales across a larger market.**

But this layer deserves more skepticism than physical bottlenecks. Software can be reproduced, bundled or replaced more quickly than a semiconductor-equipment supply chain or a high-voltage transformer factory. AI itself can reduce the cost of creating integrations. Therefore the relevant question is not whether interoperability matters; it is whether a particular company owns proprietary state, distribution, certification, trust or network effects that allow it to capture the economics.

## 21. AI Security as a strategic industry

AI does not merely create more software. It can create millions of autonomous agents, industrial control systems, autonomous cyber tools, military systems, robots and AI-mediated decisions.

That radically expands the attack surface.

The chain is straightforward:

**more AI → more autonomous action → larger attack surface → higher cost of failure → greater security demand.**

The stronger thesis is therefore not that ordinary cybersecurity spending grows. It is that **AI Security becomes a control layer for an increasingly autonomous economy**.

Israel is particularly interesting in this context because of its existing cybersecurity expertise, defense technology, intelligence ecosystem and experience operating under adversarial conditions. Real deployment in difficult environments can generate friction data that is hard to reproduce in laboratories: attacks, failures, edge cases, permission mistakes and operational constraints.

A potential loop is:

**US models and compute → Israeli security and defense deployment → adversarial failure data → better security systems → reintegration into US and allied platforms → more deployment.**

This does not mean Israel automatically wins AI Security. Large US incumbents such as Microsoft, Palo Alto Networks and CrowdStrike have enormous distribution and platform advantages. The investable question is narrower: whether specific Israeli companies possess expertise or products that become more valuable as the allied AI-security market expands, and whether that value is not already priced in.

## 22. Trusted resources and the geopolitical premium

The same accessible-supply logic applies to commodities and strategic materials.

A global supply figure can be misleading if a large share of the supply is politically inaccessible or strategically undesirable.

An Australian rare-earth producer and a Chinese rare-earth producer may have similar physical output but very different strategic value to a US-led defense or semiconductor supply chain.

The relevant market can therefore become **trusted accessible supply**, not total global supply.

This creates potential premiums for minerals, processing capacity, nuclear fuel, specialty chemicals and other inputs located inside politically trusted jurisdictions.

Australia and Canada are particularly interesting because they combine natural resources, land and relatively strong political alignment with the United States. Their role is less glamorous than frontier AI or robotics, but it may be more robust across multiple geopolitical futures. Even if allied industrial coordination remains incomplete, a desire to reduce dependence on China can increase investment in trusted resource supply. If the larger industrial expansion thesis succeeds, the demand becomes even stronger.

This is one reason Australia and Canada fit the “don’t lose money” side of the framework: the thesis does not need a perfectly coordinated alliance to create demand.

## 23. China: integrated national scaling versus distributed allied scaling

China’s greatest structural advantage is not simply population or GDP. It is industrial density and coordination.

A Chinese robotics company can exist near battery producers, motors, electronics manufacturers, machine shops, factories, logistics networks and customers. Problems discovered in production can move quickly through the supply chain. Successful products can scale into mass manufacturing. Manufacturing itself produces new knowledge.

The feedback loop can be extremely fast:

**deployment → production feedback → manufacturing improvement → lower cost → more deployment.**

The US-led system is different. It may possess a larger combined collection of frontier capabilities, but those capabilities are fragmented across sovereign states.

The strategic comparison can therefore be framed as:

> **China’s integrated national scaling machine versus America’s ability to orchestrate a larger but more fragmented trusted network.**

China begins with an integration advantage. The allied network begins with an optionality advantage. The outcome depends partly on whether the United States can reduce enough of the Coordination Discount to mobilize the network when the next bottleneck appears.

This is why the stronger Allied Scaling thesis could become strategically dangerous for China. A coordinated system that combines American AI and capital, Japanese manufacturing, Korean electronics and memory, Dutch semiconductor equipment, allied energy and minerals, Israeli security capability and European industrial expertise is a much more formidable competitor than any member considered alone.

But this outcome should not be assumed. China can coordinate industrial policy faster. Democratic alliances must negotiate, legislate, fund, certify and maintain political support across election cycles. Execution speed remains one of the strongest arguments against the thesis.

The investment framework must therefore work even if the geopolitical architecture develops only partially.

## 24. A synthetic balance sheet for the allied economy

The long-term research project is to treat the allied network as a synthetic economy and build something like a strategic balance sheet.

On the asset side, map the capabilities that already exist: frontier AI, cloud, chip design, lithography, memory, robotics, power equipment, nuclear, cybersecurity, defense systems, industrial automation, minerals and logistics.

On the liability side, map the unresolved constraints: China dependence, single points of failure, grid limitations, insufficient production capacity, permitting delays, incompatible standards, missing manufacturing skills, national protectionism and weak cross-border procurement.

Then ask several questions for each node.

What does the network still need from China? Which dependency would become unacceptable in a crisis? Which allied country already possesses a credible substitute? Which capability is technically available but artificially small because its home market is limited? Which bottleneck would receive the largest demand increase if Physical AI or defense production accelerates? Which company owns the bottleneck? Can it scale without destroying its own ROIC? What does the current valuation already assume?

The result should not be a map of geopolitical winners. It should be a map of **investable scarcity**.

## 25. Three broad classes of investment

The framework naturally produces three broad types of potential investment.

The first are **Node Owners**: companies controlling a scarce capability that the broader network cannot easily replace. Semiconductor equipment is the cleanest example. The attraction is greatest when both efficient specialization and redundant capacity expansion increase demand for the same node.

The second are **Network Infrastructure** companies: businesses enabling the nodes to operate together. This can include secure communications, cybersecurity, electrical infrastructure, logistics, industrial software, testing, certification and data integration. These companies are attractive when their economics do not depend on guessing which final product wins.

The third are **Coordination Beneficiaries**: companies whose technology already exists but whose scale is artificially limited by fragmented national markets. If procurement and standards become more interoperable, their accessible TAM can expand much faster than their technology changes.

The third category may contain the largest hidden reratings. The first category may contain the most robust bottlenecks. The second may offer the best “picks and shovels” exposure.

## 26. Country research priorities

The countries should not be ranked simply by whether their stock markets are attractive. They should be ranked by how useful their capabilities are to the broader thesis and how likely public companies are to capture the value.

Japan is currently the highest-priority research market outside the United States. The combination of strategic proximity to the US, robotics, machine tools, precision manufacturing, sensors, materials and semiconductor equipment makes it the cleanest test of the Physical AI specialization thesis. The key question is whether US intelligence can combine with Japanese embodiment in a way that materially changes the economics of Japanese industrial companies.

The Netherlands rank highly because a very small country already controls extraordinary semiconductor-equipment capability. The investment universe is concentrated, but the country is almost a real-world prototype of the thesis: a small national specialization can become systemically important when it serves a much larger technological network.

Israel is attractive because it may have the highest asymmetry in AI Security and defense technology. Its domestic market is small relative to the potential allied market, so accessible-TAM expansion could matter enormously. The trade-off is geopolitical risk and a smaller public-company universe.

South Korea is essential to memory, electronics, batteries, shipbuilding and mass manufacturing. It may become increasingly important as AI moves into the physical economy, although some of its most important industries are cyclical and its economic relationship with China is more complex.

Australia and Canada are important as trusted resource, energy and land nodes. They may offer less spectacular narratives but greater robustness across geopolitical scenarios.

The United Kingdom is deeply integrated into the US security architecture and has strong aerospace, defense, research and financial capability.

France and Germany remain technologically important, but the investment thesis should be more selective because both political economy and strategic-autonomy considerations can make the coordination trade less clean. The rule for these markets should be simple: **the company should still be attractive even if transatlantic integration advances slowly.**

## 28. Current highest-information research candidates

The current priority list is not a list of automatic buys. It is a list of companies whose analysis would teach the most about whether the larger framework is economically real.

**Eaton** is the cleanest test of the robust electrical bottleneck. AI datacenters, fabs, defense factories, robotics factories and reshoring all require electrical infrastructure. If allied coordination succeeds, capacity expands. If coordination fails and countries build redundant national capacity, electrical demand may still increase. The key question is not whether electrification is real; it is whether the duration and magnitude of demand stacking remain underestimated relative to valuation.

**Quanta Services** tests whether the real bottleneck is not equipment but execution. Capital and transformers are useless until transmission, substations and connections are actually built. If skilled infrastructure execution is scarce, Quanta may sit directly in the conversion layer between theoretical power supply and usable power.

**FANUC** is one of the most interesting tests of the Physical AI thesis. The possible transition is from deterministic industrial robots toward AI-perceptive, adaptive physical execution systems. If AI makes the “brain” cheaper and more general, the scarce layer may become motors, controls, reliability, safety, integration knowledge and installed industrial infrastructure. This is similar to the UiPath transition thesis in the physical world: the technology threatening the old implementation may increase the value of the existing execution infrastructure.

**Check Point** tests the Israel and AI-Security thesis. The possible mispricing would be that a highly profitable but mature cybersecurity company is still valued as structurally slow while AI creates a larger security surface that could reaccelerate growth. The comparison against Palo Alto Networks, CrowdStrike and Microsoft is essential. A strong macro thesis about AI Security is useless if a different company captures the value.

**SK hynix** tests whether HBM is a normal memory cycle or a structurally scarcer AI input. The key investor uncertainty is whether current extraordinary economics are peak-cycle earnings or evidence that memory’s strategic role has changed. If HBM remains difficult to produce and demand per AI accelerator keeps rising, historical memory-cycle valuation frameworks may understate the company’s long-run economics. If supply normalizes quickly, a low-looking multiple could simply be a peak-earnings illusion.

**ASML** is the cleanest example of a scarce Node Owner. AI compute growth, advanced semiconductor demand and strategically redundant fabs can all increase lithography demand. The difficulty is that almost every sophisticated investor already understands ASML’s importance. The edge must therefore come from forecasting the magnitude and duration of the capacity cycle better than consensus, not merely recognizing the chokepoint.

**Cameco** tests the Trusted Nuclear thesis across uranium, fuel services and reactor infrastructure. The investment case must go beyond “nuclear is coming back.” The question is whether some part of the trusted fuel and reactor chain remains structurally scarcer than the market assumes.

**Elbit Systems** is perhaps the cleanest Accessible-TAM case. The question is whether an Israeli national specialist can increasingly operate as an alliance-scale supplier and whether international demand grows faster than the capacity and valuation assumptions already embedded in the stock.

**GE Vernova** tests the broad electricity-capacity thesis. It is attractive because generation, grid and electrification can all benefit from the same industrial expansion. It is dangerous because capital-intensive industries can absorb huge demand without producing equally attractive shareholder returns. The analysis must focus on pricing power, margins and ROIC rather than simply backlog.

**Schneider Electric** combines energy management and industrial automation, making it particularly interesting if Physical AI causes power systems, factory control and digital automation to converge. The question is whether the combined system becomes more valuable than the segments considered separately and whether the market already prices that convergence.

## 29. Research timing and the anti-FOMO rule

The entire thesis does not need to be researched in a week.

Different layers are at different stages.

The energy, grid, AI-infrastructure and HBM portions are already active. These deserve immediate screening because current valuations can change quickly and the first demand wave is visible.

Physical AI, Japanese industrial specialization and semiconductor equipment should be researched now, but the full economic impact may unfold over years rather than weeks.

Accessible-TAM expansion, smaller national champions, standards, trusted resources and the stronger distributed-state architecture are likely to provide more evidence over the coming months and years.

A useful working timeline is:

**2026–2028:** AI power, datacenter and grid expansion already active.

**2026–2029:** defense expansion and redundant industrial capacity accelerating.

**2027–2030:** more meaningful allied procurement and coordination could become visible.

**2027–2031:** US-Japan Physical AI and manufacturing integration could become increasingly relevant.

**approximately 2028–2033:** possible major industrial Physical AI acceleration.

**2028–2035+:** stronger forms of a distributed Scaling State, if the thesis succeeds.

These are research windows, not fixed predictions.

The most important rule is not to confuse urgency with the need to buy immediately. A decade-long structural cycle can produce many bottleneck migrations and many entry points. Missing the first 20 percent of one stock does not mean missing the thesis.

The larger mistake would be buying dozens of partially understood companies because the world appears to be moving quickly.

The objective is to build the map early enough that, when evidence and valuation align, capital can be deployed with confidence.

## 30. How every company should be analyzed from here

The structural framework is only useful if it eventually reaches shareholder returns.

Every company should therefore be forced through the same chain.

First, identify the exact bottleneck it owns. “AI infrastructure” is not specific enough. Is the company supplying switchgear, skilled electrical construction, HBM, lithography, trusted uranium enrichment, machine vision, robotic controls, certification, or something else?

Second, determine whether the bottleneck is actually difficult to substitute or expand. A fast-growing market with easy supply response may create enormous revenue but poor returns.

Third, map the causal path from the structural thesis to financial statements:

**structural change → customer behavior → orders → backlog → utilization → revenue → pricing → margins → free cash flow → shareholder return.**

Fourth, compare the company against the strongest alternative. Why Eaton rather than Schneider or Hubbell? Why FANUC rather than Yaskawa or Keyence? Why Check Point rather than Palo Alto or CrowdStrike? Why SK hynix rather than Micron or Samsung? Why ASML rather than a different semiconductor-equipment company?

Fifth, determine what the market already expects. A company can be a perfect structural beneficiary and still be a bad investment if the valuation requires nearly perfect execution.

Sixth, identify the central investor uncertainty. Is the market worried about cyclicality, capex returns, competition, political risk, commoditization, margins or timing? The opportunity exists only if our probability distribution differs from the one implied by price for a defensible reason.

Seventh, define kill conditions before the position is made. If the thesis depends on a bottleneck staying scarce, what evidence would show that supply has caught up? If the thesis depends on Physical AI, what evidence would show that the economics are not improving? If the thesis depends on accessible-TAM expansion, what evidence would show that procurement remains nationally fragmented?

Finally, compare expected returns with the S&P 500 and the best available alternative. A good company is not enough. The position must be a good use of scarce capital.

## 31. What should not be assumed

Several mistakes would destroy the usefulness of this framework.

AI growth does not automatically mean an AI-related stock is attractive.

Industry growth does not automatically produce shareholder returns.

Strategic importance does not automatically create pricing power.

Government support does not automatically produce high ROIC.

Redundancy does not automatically make the redundant producer profitable.

A monopoly-like node is not automatically cheap.

A correct geopolitical thesis does not guarantee the correct security selection.

A correct long-term future does not guarantee correct timing.

The entire point of the framework is to make these distinctions explicit.

Every macro claim must eventually collide with the financial question: **who captures the value, at what return on capital, and at what price am I buying it?**

## 32. The broader Scaling State idea

The Allied Scaling Network is ultimately an extension of a broader idea about state capacity.

A country can possess abundant physical and human resources while failing to convert them into output because institutional bottlenecks block the conversion. Land can exist without housing. Energy can exist without grid connections. Capital can exist without factories. Talent can exist without institutions capable of scaling its output.

The original Scaling State question is therefore:

> **How can a country turn more of its existing potential into actual productive capacity?**

The AI-capacity question adds another layer:

> **How can technological capability be converted into energy, factories, robots, infrastructure and deployment quickly enough?**

The Allied Scaling Network adds a third:

> **How can the United States convert not only its own resources, but the specialized resources of a trusted international network into usable capacity?**

The resulting system can be represented as:

**AI capability → more R&D and economic possibilities → new bottlenecks → identify the strongest trusted capability for each bottleneck → demand and capital → specialized capacity expansion → deployment → real-world feedback → stronger specialist ecosystems → greater combined capacity → more economic output → more capital and capability → new expansion.**

The network begins to scale itself.

## 33. The central investment hypothesis

The strongest version of the thesis is not that the allied world becomes perfectly coordinated.

It is that AI and geopolitical competition increase the value of converting technological capability into physical capacity, while the US-led world already contains much of the required capability in fragmented form. Selective coordination can unlock some of that latent capacity. Failed coordination can simultaneously create redundant national investment. Both pathways can increase demand for bottlenecks that are difficult to scale.

This produces a particularly attractive class of assets:

> **scarce infrastructure whose demand rises both when allied coordination works and when political fragmentation causes countries to duplicate capacity.**

Electrical equipment is one candidate. Grid construction may be another. Semiconductor equipment can have this property. Certain industrial components, energy systems, trusted materials and security layers may also qualify.

This is why the investment problem should not be framed as “Which country wins?” or even “Which industry wins?”

The better question is:

> **As AI and geopolitical competition force the advanced allied world to convert more technological capability into real physical capacity, which bottlenecks become unavoidable, which companies own them, and where is the market still underestimating the magnitude, duration or economic consequences of that transition?**

That is the current research frontier.

## 34. Final perspective

The most important geopolitical unit of the AI era may not always be the nation-state considered in isolation. It may increasingly be the **coordinated capability network**.

China possesses a major structural advantage because much of its manufacturing system already exists inside one dense national industrial ecosystem. The United States possesses a different potential advantage: it sits at the center of a much larger constellation of wealthy, technologically advanced allies whose capabilities are highly complementary.

The weakness is fragmentation.

The opportunity is selective integration.

If the United States and its allies reduce even part of their Coordination Discount, pool demand where it matters, make private capital more willing to finance strategic capacity, connect specialized production nodes and preserve redundancy at existential chokepoints, the effective productive capacity of the network can rise faster than conventional national accounting suggests.

Japan can become more valuable as Physical AI makes industrial embodiment scarce. Korea can become more valuable as memory and electronics become central to AI systems. The Netherlands can remain disproportionately important through semiconductor equipment. Israel can become more valuable if AI Security becomes a strategic control layer. Canada and Australia can gain importance as trusted energy and resource bases. European industrial and energy capabilities can remain important even when political integration is incomplete.

The United States does not need to dominate every node.

Its deeper strategic advantage may be the ability to orchestrate enough of them.

If that happens, the competition with China changes. It is no longer simply America versus China. It becomes China’s integrated national scaling machine versus America’s ability to transform the world’s most powerful alliance network into a partially integrated scaling machine of its own.

That second machine already possesses many of the pieces.

The central question is whether the pieces become sufficiently connected — and, for investors, which scarce assets get paid whether they connect efficiently or are built redundantly.
