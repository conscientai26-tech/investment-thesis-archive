# My Investment Record and Alpha Reassessment
**Record through October 14, 2026 · Written October 15, 2026**

## A correction to my earlier performance assessments

I need to correct how I have described my investment performance. **My previous alpha estimates were probably wrong, or at least not supported by a complete and consistent calculation.** I have previously recorded figures such as +5.7 percentage points in August, +3.1 points in September and +6.7 points on October 9. Other attempts to reconstruct the portfolio have produced conflicting figures, including +4.0 and +11.9 points. Those numbers should not be treated as independently verified results.

This does not mean the underlying investment decisions never happened or that their dates should be changed. It means I have not yet established a sufficiently reliable method for measuring the returns of all of them together. My record includes partial exits, recycled capital, two different portfolio sizing conventions, positions denominated in other currencies, and some historical reference prices that are missing or inconsistent with market data. Any one of these can distort an alpha calculation. Combined, they make previous estimates especially vulnerable to error.

I am keeping the original records intact rather than rewriting my earlier judgments to fit the latest calculation. This document separates what I decided, what the portfolio journal actually records and what the performance calculations currently suggest. The investment record is real as a record of **virtual decisions**, not a brokerage account or independently audited track record.

## How I arrived at the October 14 alpha estimate

The numbers in the earlier checkpoints don't line up with the newer calculation. That is real, and I would rather leave the discrepancy visible than quietly change the old files. The August checkpoint mentioned roughly +5.7 percentage points, September roughly +3.1, and the October 9 interim checkpoint +6.7. Later attempts produced +4.0 and +11.9. The latest reconstruction produces **$7,445.45 of dollar outperformance**, expressed as **10.18% relative to average capital employed** through October 14. I can show how that number was calculated. I cannot yet claim that it is a fully verified, standard time-weighted alpha.

A large part of the confusion came from treating my repository as if it were already a clean trade ledger. It isn't. It contains decisions written at different times, reference prices, estimates of portfolio performance, and a change in the sizing framework. It also contains sales and subsequent purchases that reused capital. Those facts are easy to lose when a model reads a monthly summary and tries to reconstruct an entire portfolio from it.

The calculation was rebuilt from the original transactions rather than from the latest list of holdings. I used 17 measurable purchase lots. That includes the two Amazon purchases separately, and splits the August 10 Alphabet purchase into the shares sold on August 26 and the shares kept afterward. The full ServiceNow and Xiaomi exits are included. The July Alphabet pilot is acknowledged but excluded numerically because I have not recovered its initial size or cost. My original Microsoft investment was $600; for comparability with the later $1 million reference portfolio, it is shown as $6,000, without pretending that I made another purchase.

The cumulative value of those normalized purchases is **$184,000**. This is not the amount of capital I had working throughout the period. Some of it was invested for only a few days; some was still invested in October; some was sold and reused. That difference explains why dividing my cumulative profit by $184,000 is not a sensible substitute for tracking capital actually deployed over time.

### Reconstructing the positions and the benchmark

For each lot I calculated the gain or loss from its recorded entry reference to its recorded sale price, or to the October 14 market close if it was still open. I then calculated what the same amount of money would have earned in the S&P 500 price index over that **same window**. When I sold Alphabet shares on August 26, the benchmark for those shares ended on August 26. It did not carry on accumulating returns through October while the stock's result was frozen at the sale price.

In simple terms, for an investment of `K` dollars, the stock's profit is `K × (exit price / entry price − 1)`. The comparison investment's profit is `K × (S&P exit / S&P entry − 1)`. I subtract the second from the first. That gives the lot's excess profit in dollars. For positions priced outside the US, the stock performance must be translated into dollars before making that comparison.

This method can give a positive result on an investment that lost money. The August Alphabet shares sold at $342 are a useful example. Their recorded purchase price was $344.98, so I lost around **$370.92** on the portion sold. But the equivalent S&P 500 position would have lost around **$428.72** over the same window. The relative difference is about **+$57.80**. Losing less than the index is still positive benchmark-relative performance. Likewise, a profitable stock can detract from alpha if the index performed even better.

Microsoft shows the other side. Using the $6,000 normalized lot and the reconstructed $393.82 entry reference, the October 14 value at approximately $533.99 gives a stock profit of roughly **$2,135.54**. The equivalent S&P 500 price-index position, measured from the July 17 session used for the Saturday July 18 decision to the October 14 close, gained about **$210.19**. The difference is approximately **+$1,925.36** in Microsoft's favor.

### Checking the calculation against the cash ledger

I also reconstructed the value of the account without calculating alpha one stock at a time. On the normalized basis, the purchases add up to **$184,000.00**. Proceeds from the three earlier sales add up to about **$70,020.98**: roughly $42,568.10 from the Alphabet reduction, $12,625.79 from ServiceNow and $14,827.09 from Xiaomi. The open holdings were valued at approximately **$121,452.97** at the October 14 close.

The profit therefore works out as:

`$70,020.98 + $121,452.97 − $184,000.00 = $7,473.95`

This agrees with the sum of the stock-level gains and losses. The matched benchmark lots produced approximately **$28.49**, leaving **$7,445.45** of dollar outperformance after rounding at the underlying calculation level.

This was an important check because one of the earlier calculations had written the ledger equation incorrectly, mixing purchase amounts and sale proceeds in a way that did not even add up. A correct ledger is basic, but it matters. It means that realized proceeds and shares still held are both included, and that sold capital is not simply forgotten. It is **not** a second independent audit of every historical stock price: if the same inaccurate price goes into both calculations, both calculations can still agree.

### Where the 10.18% comes from

Dollar outperformance and percentage outperformance are different things. My virtual reference portfolio has a nominal size of **$1 million**, but I have not invested that entire amount. If I divide $7,445.45 by $1 million, the contribution is about **0.74% of total reference capital**. That is a legitimate description of the complete notional account, but it is not a measure of the performance of the capital I actually allocated to these stock selections.

Using $184,000 as the denominator has the opposite problem. It counts cumulative purchases regardless of whether the same money was subsequently freed and invested again, or how long each position was held. That would yield about **4.05%** and would describe a different quantity.

In this reconstruction, I used a capital-time denominator instead. The period from July 18 to October 14 covers **88 calendar days**. I multiplied each lot's cost by the number of days it was held, summed those capital-days, and divided by 88. Microsoft's normalized $6,000 position contributes $6,000 multiplied by 88 days. The Alphabet shares sold in August contribute approximately $42,939 multiplied by only 16 days. The retained Alphabet shares continue through the valuation date. The same logic applies to every other lot.

On the day-count conventions used in the audit, this produced an **average capital-at-work figure of $73,158.96**.

The final calculation is:

`$7,445.45 / $73,158.96 × 100 = 10.18%`

That is the exact origin of the figure. Nothing has been added to the returns to reach ten percent. It is the recorded-reference dollar outperformance divided by the model's average allocated capital over the observation window.

I need to be precise about its name. This is **capital-time-normalized excess performance**, not an independently established time-weighted return series. I should not call it conventional **10.18 percentage points of portfolio alpha** without also describing the denominator and method. A daily time-weighted comparison, or a cash-flow-matched money-weighted return calculation, would answer related but different questions.

### Why the earlier GitHub estimates disagree

The October 9 checkpoint described +6.7 percentage points as **owner-reported**, not independently recalculated from every transaction. Its language was appropriately cautious, but the number was later repeated as though it were already verified. That was a mistake.

The later +4.0 result mixed the total of all purchases with capital that had actually been working in the portfolio, and allowed benchmark exposure to continue past certain sales. That can materially distort the comparison. The subsequent +11.9 result improved the treatment of exits and capital timing, but it still relied on wrong or uncertain historical inputs. It also used the custom capital-time denominator without adequately separating that statistic from standard portfolio alpha.

The clearest input problem was **SAP**. An earlier reconstruction assigned SAP the **$206.09** August 19 closing reference associated with **Salesforce**. A subsequent check put the SAP US-listed share at approximately **$215.60** on that date. Using a lower purchase price had overstated SAP's return. For the October 14 reconstruction, the SAP lot uses $215.60 at entry and approximately $220.41 at the valuation date; its estimated excess profit falls to about **+$261.10**. The fact that a single price error could change the result is why I stopped trusting the older headline figures.

Wolters Kluwer is another issue. It trades in euros, but I measure performance in dollars. The reference model uses approximately **€68.86** at entry, **€78.06** at the October 14 valuation, and EUR/USD rates of roughly **1.1622** and **1.1195**. Simply using the euro share-price change as the dollar return would exaggerate what a USD investor earned. Xiaomi is recorded in Hong Kong dollars; the current reconstruction treats the HKD/USD conversion as approximately constant. That is an assumption that still needs a source-level check.

There is also a date difference. The October 9 recorded-reference reconstruction came out to approximately **$7,619.53 of dollar alpha**, or **10.77% on its capital-time denominator**. The October 14 model gives **$7,445.45**, or **10.18%**. Prices and the index moved between the two market closes. They are not estimates for the same day, so they should not be expected to match exactly.

Finally, the decisions made on October 14 are not missing from the history. Cadence's proposed exit, the Synopsys reduction, the four smaller trims and the three increases are documented in the section above. They are not treated as confirmed transactions because the source documents do not record executions. Recording a decision and recognizing a realized gain are different things. The decisions can affect my future return; they should not silently change what I had already earned by the October 14 close.

### The limitation I cannot ignore

The most serious remaining problem is **Alphabet's August 10 entry**. The original journal uses **$344.98**, but a historical price check indicated an August 10 close around **$357.52**, with the stock trading above the recorded reference throughout that session. If the market never offered $344.98 on the stated purchase day, that is a valid paper-trade reference only in the sense that it is what I wrote down; it is not evidence of a realistically executable purchase. I am preserving the original number for record integrity and reporting the effect of more conservative execution assumptions separately.

Repricing the Alphabet purchase to the August 10 closing price changes the calculation substantially. An alternative simulation that reprices all purchases to available trading-session closing prices produces about **$5,299 of dollar alpha**, or **7.27% on the same type of capital-time denominator**, rather than $7,445 and 10.18%. That is not a discrepancy to hide. It tells me how much of the reported outperformance depends on the reference-price convention.

I also still lack the cost and size of the original July Alphabet pilot, and some historical references for ServiceNow, Salesforce and Wolters Kluwer remain reconstructed. The benchmark is the S&P 500 **price index**, excluding reinvested dividends. Those omissions and conventions can change a fully comparable return calculation.

### What I am willing to claim

The **10.18% result is arithmetically reproducible under the documented recorded-reference assumptions**. I can identify the lots, trace the stock profits and benchmark profits, reconcile the sales and remaining shares, and show the denominator used to turn dollars into a percentage. In that limited, explicit sense, the earlier unexplained performance estimates have been superseded by a better reconstruction.

What is **not** yet settled is my exact economically realizable or standard time-weighted portfolio alpha. A model based on actual available session prices gives a lower number, and unresolved historical data still matter. Calling the 10.18% figure fully audited would repeat the same mistake that caused the earlier confusion.

I would rather have a precise record of what is known than a confident number I cannot defend. If my original investment judgments were good, that should remain apparent after the execution assumptions, currencies, dividends and methodology are all handled correctly. That, rather than protecting a particular percentage, is the reason for keeping this archive.

## What I want to learn

The purpose of this journal is not to defend a particular performance figure. I want to know whether my original expectations about businesses were better than the expectations implied by market prices, and whether the choices I made about sizing, exiting and reinvesting improved the result.

A business can develop exactly as I expected and still be a poor investment if its starting valuation was too high. I can also select good companies and destroy the benefit by selling them too soon or reallocating the proceeds badly. These are separate questions and should be measured separately.

The record is young. It covers less than three months from my first documented purchases to the October 14 checkpoint. Even a genuinely strong short-term result would not by itself establish a durable investment edge. What would matter more is whether a larger number of dated predictions and decisions remain demonstrably successful over a longer period.

Going forward, I will preserve the original investment decisions, explicitly mark reconstructed data, keep realized and unrealized profit separate, and re-run the benchmark comparison using consistent price, dividend, currency and cash-flow conventions. Earlier performance estimates remain part of the historical record, but **they should not be cited as reliable measures of alpha until that reconciliation is complete**.
