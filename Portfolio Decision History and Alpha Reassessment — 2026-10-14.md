# My Investment Record and Alpha Reassessment
**Record through October 14, 2026 · Written October 15, 2026**

## A correction to my earlier performance assessments

I need to correct how I have described my investment performance. **My previous alpha estimates were probably wrong, or at least not supported by a complete and consistent calculation.** I have previously recorded figures such as +5.7 percentage points in August, +3.1 points in September and +6.7 points on October 9. Other attempts to reconstruct the portfolio have produced conflicting figures, including +4.0 and +11.9 points. Those numbers should not be treated as independently verified results.

This does not mean the underlying investment decisions never happened or that their dates should be changed. It means I have not yet established a sufficiently reliable method for measuring the returns of all of them together. My record includes partial exits, recycled capital, two different portfolio sizing conventions, positions denominated in other currencies, and some historical reference prices that are missing or inconsistent with market data. Any one of these can distort an alpha calculation. Combined, they make previous estimates especially vulnerable to error.

I am keeping the original records intact rather than rewriting my earlier judgments to fit the latest calculation. This document separates what I decided, what the portfolio journal actually records and what the performance calculations currently suggest. The investment record is real as a record of **virtual decisions**, not a brokerage account or independently audited track record.

## What my alpha calculations currently indicate

My benchmark is the S&P 500. To judge individual stock selection, the appropriate starting point is to match each investment lot with a benchmark investment over the **same holding period**. When a stock is sold, its benchmark comparison ends at that sale, not at a later checkpoint. Reinvested capital cannot be counted as if the earlier investment were still open. Open lots are measured to a common valuation date.

My previous numbers did not consistently follow these rules, and some input prices were wrong. A particularly concrete example is **SAP**: an earlier calculation assigned it the **$206.09** August 19 entry price used for Salesforce, whereas a subsequent historical-price check suggested **$215.60 for SAP's U.S.-listed shares**. The prices and market conventions still need final verification, but the discrepancy alone makes the earlier result unreliable.

A subsequent, still provisional reconstruction produced the following **illustrative** results:

| Valuation date and basis | Estimated dollar excess return | Estimated capital-time-normalized excess return |
|---|---:|---:|
| October 9, recorded-reference reconstruction | +$7,619.53 | +10.77 percentage points |
| October 14, recorded-reference reconstruction | +$7,445.45 | +10.18 percentage points |
| October 14, hypothetical trading-session-price reconstruction | +$5,299 approximately | +7.27 percentage points approximately |

**None of these numbers should be described as a verified or audited portfolio alpha.** They are outputs of different assumptions. The October 14 reference reconstruction previously reported approximately **$184,000 of cumulative normalized purchases, $70,020.98 in prior sale proceeds, $121,452.97 of remaining market value and $7,473.95 of total profit**, compared with approximately **$28.49 of matched-window S&P 500 price-index profit**. Those figures were reported as a reconciliation, but both the historical input series and the treatment of capital flows still require independent validation. A superficially balanced ledger is not enough to prove that the performance attribution is correct.

The percentages above use a **capital-time-normalized** denominator, approximately the average invested principal over the period. This is a custom measurement of dollar outperformance relative to capital employed. It is **not** the same as time-weighted portfolio alpha, and neither should be confused with return on the full $1 million virtual reference portfolio. Dividends, foreign-exchange conventions, stock adjustments and unverified entry prices could change the results further.

The earlier Alphabet pilot lot is missing from these numerical calculations because its original amount and price are unresolved. Microsoft is treated as $6,000 for normalized comparisons while preserving its actual historical $600 pilot size. The 2026 market-price series, especially Alphabet's August entry, should be tested against realistically available execution prices, rather than accepting every recorded paper-trade reference as tradable.

I therefore regard the numerical results as **working estimates**. The most defensible statement today is that the existing record suggests meaningful outperformance under some assumptions, but **my previous alpha assessments were probably inaccurate, and my true comparable alpha has not been established to an auditable standard**.

## What I want to learn

The purpose of this journal is not to defend a particular performance figure. I want to know whether my original expectations about businesses were better than the expectations implied by market prices, and whether the choices I made about sizing, exiting and reinvesting improved the result.

A business can develop exactly as I expected and still be a poor investment if its starting valuation was too high. I can also select good companies and destroy the benefit by selling them too soon or reallocating the proceeds badly. These are separate questions and should be measured separately.

The record is young. It covers less than three months from my first documented purchases to the October 14 checkpoint. Even a genuinely strong short-term result would not by itself establish a durable investment edge. What would matter more is whether a larger number of dated predictions and decisions remain demonstrably successful over a longer period.

Going forward, I will preserve the original investment decisions, explicitly mark reconstructed data, keep realized and unrealized profit separate, and re-run the benchmark comparison using consistent price, dividend, currency and cash-flow conventions. Earlier performance estimates remain part of the historical record, but **they should not be cited as reliable measures of alpha until that reconciliation is complete**.
