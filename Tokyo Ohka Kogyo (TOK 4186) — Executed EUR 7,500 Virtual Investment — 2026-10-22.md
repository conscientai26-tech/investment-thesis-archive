## Why I bought it

I spent several days looking at Japanese semiconductor companies because I wanted exposure to the physical bottlenecks created by expanding AI computing—not simply another company whose sales depend on the popularity of an AI application.

Tokyo Ohka Kogyo is a specialist in photoresists and extremely pure process chemicals used in semiconductor manufacturing. It is not a chip designer and it is not a semiconductor foundry. I like that distinction. When the industry experiments with new chips, memory architectures and manufacturing processes, somebody must still provide materials that work reliably at extraordinarily small tolerances. TOK has spent decades building the formulations, manufacturing quality and customer relationships required to supply those processes.

That does not make the shares cheap. In fact, an important part of this decision is that **I bought despite not having proved a large discount to intrinsic value**. I am taking a measured position in a company that I think has a credible route to becoming more economically important, while recognizing that a significant amount of growth is already expected.

The case rests on three connected propositions:

1. The rise in semiconductor complexity and advanced packaging creates additional profitable material requirements. This matters even if the number of chips grows less quickly than AI spending.
2. TOK is in a position to win some of the new material qualifications rather than simply defend its existing products, because it already works inside demanding manufacturing processes and is investing in next-generation resists and packaging materials.
3. The new capacity being built in Japan, South Korea and elsewhere will eventually produce substantially more cash than it costs to maintain—*without* requiring a new investment of comparable size for every increment of sales.

The third proposition is the one I trust least today, and therefore the one I will monitor most closely. Industrial relevance is not the same thing as shareholder returns.

## What the business really controls

TOK's central asset is the ability to supply carefully formulated materials that customers can integrate into highly sensitive, high-yield manufacturing processes. A photoresist that performs well in a laboratory is not automatically usable in high-volume manufacturing. The manufacturer must be confident about resolution, pattern fidelity, defect rates, stability across batches, sensitivity, the effect on yield and the reliability of delivery.

This creates switching costs. Once a process is qualified, replacing a supplier is not a casual purchasing decision. It requires more experiments, engineering time and production risk. The accumulated knowledge from joint development also gives established suppliers an advantage when new requirements appear.

But it is a **conditional moat**, not a monopoly. TOK's estimated 2025 EUV photoresist share was about 29.3% by shipped volume in the market research cited in the research archive. That says something meaningful about existing competitive position; it does not imply that TOK controls all EUV materials, that market share is permanent, or that it captures 29.3% of the profit pool. Its position in ArF is materially smaller. Product, customer, node and metric definitions matter.

The real test is whether it can win the next generation of qualifications. Management said in its February 2026 results Q&A that evaluation for leading-edge nodes was underway and it expected higher share in the next generation than in the preceding one. Management also discussed its own MOR and SMR development. These are encouraging comments but not independent proof of future wins. I will judge them against later production evidence, not repeat them as facts already realized.

I looked at Tokyo Electron, SCREEN, Shin-Etsu and SUMCO as alternative ways to own Japanese semiconductor bottlenecks. Tokyo Electron appears exceptionally protected in coater/developer equipment and is much broader in manufacturing tools. SCREEN has interesting advanced-cleaning and bonding opportunities. Shin-Etsu is more diversified across materials. SUMCO has demanding technology but illustrates how a difficult-to-enter wafer business can still deliver poor returns on capital during a bad capacity cycle. I chose TOK because its combination of qualified consumables, packaging expansion, and participation in new materials offered the particular type of upside I wanted at this stage. I have *not* proved that it is superior to those companies on a valuation-adjusted basis.

## The most interesting expansion is not just more EUV

The obvious narrative is that more advanced chips require more photoresist. I do not think that is enough for an investment thesis. It is widely understood and therefore likely partly in the share price.

The more interesting question is whether TOK can supply **additional materials and process steps per advanced chip**, especially in HBM and advanced packaging. As chips become more tightly stacked and integrated, wafer handling, temporary bonding and debonding, redistribution layers, bumping and processing of fragile structures can become more demanding. TOK has relevant materials in several of these processes.

Management's product-level forecast for Wafer Handling System (WHS) materials pointed to roughly **700% sales growth from 2024 to 2027**. That is an eightfold level after a 700% increase, but it is a growth rate for a **specific, smaller business**, not for the group. A small starting base can make percentage growth look spectacular without contributing an equally spectacular amount of group profit. The absolute revenue and margins matter more.

I will therefore track whether WHS and packaging become a larger **absolute** source of earnings, and whether TOK can sell multiple high-value consumables into the same AI chip's production chain. If one customer must buy several qualified TOK products to manufacture a more complex device, TOK may capture more economic value per chip even without a proportional increase in global wafer starts.

This is the route by which my earlier "bottleneck migration" thesis could become an identifiable earnings mechanism. It is not yet a demonstrated forecast.

## The collaborations are more concrete than I originally assumed

The recently announced agreement with **AIMECHATEC** matters because it connects a materials supplier to a manufacturer of production equipment and process integration. On October 20 TOK announced that the two companies would form **Zero Newton Solutions**, with **51% TOK / 49% AIMECHATEC** ownership, scheduled for establishment on October 31. Its remit includes joint development, evaluation and field engineering for advanced 3D and panel-level packaging.

The commercial logic is straightforward. A process jointly optimized around a machine and a consumable material may give the supplier a better chance of participating when customers move to high-volume production. It could also generate recurring material purchases after machinery has been installed.

The limits are just as important. The new joint venture begins with a very small capital base; it is not a signed order for billions of yen in materials, and management expects little immediate earnings impact. AIMECHATEC's machinery backlog is **not** TOK revenue. Counting both machinery sales and assumed material sales before customer qualification would be an obvious error.

**US-JOINT** offers a different advantage. TOK joined a twelve-company Japanese-American consortium that began full-scale operations in April 2026 in Union City, California, to develop and evaluate next-generation packaging. I see a possible information and qualification advantage: an early participant learns what tomorrow's process needs while helping to define it. But consortium membership gives no exclusive customer contract, and several participants could compete in adjacent technologies.

I also regard TOK's February 2026 investment and development partnership with **Irresistible Materials** as strategically important. The patented Multi-Trigger Resist (MTR) platform is being developed for low-NA and high-NA EUV. This is not merely an extra product. It is one way for TOK to participate in technological change that might otherwise erode the relevance of conventional resist platforms.

The competing case is real. **JSR/Inpria and Lam Research** announced a non-exclusive collaboration in September 2025 covering metal-oxide and dry-resist approaches, including Lam's Aether technology. Lam and imec expanded work on dry resist and advanced integration in October 2026. A successful alternative process could move future value away from TOK's incumbent recipes. TOK's partnerships may reduce that risk, but do not eliminate it; MTR must still prove itself in commercial manufacturing.

The industrial-alliance thesis is also becoming less speculative at the *policy* level. Japan and the United States announced a New York mature-node semiconductor project in October 2026. The Japanese foreign ministry **specifically named TOK as interested in supplying materials and components**. That is a documented prospective channel, not a signed sales contract. The US-led Pax Silica discussions and overlapping Japanese, American, Korean and European supply-chain initiatives indicate more coordination, but governments do not dictate all private purchasing choices. Some of the coordination may also encourage second-source suppliers and increase competition.

My view is that **these partnerships improve the odds of TOK being in the conversation for future processes; they do not establish how much extra money TOK will make**.

## Why the new factories matter—and why I am not treating them as free growth

On October 8 TOK completed a new photoresist manufacturing building at **Koriyama**, built at a cost of approximately **¥23 billion**, with operations scheduled for November 2026. On September 15 it also announced completion of the new **Pyeongtaek** high-purity-chemicals plant in South Korea, costing approximately **¥12 billion**, with operations planned for the first half of 2027.

Both disclosures say that the expected financial impact was **already included in TOK's August 6 guidance**. Their existence is not an additional surprise to be added to published forecasts.

The 2026 plan includes roughly **¥35.6 billion of capital investments**, approximately **¥11.6 billion of depreciation including goodwill**, and approximately **¥19.2 billion of R&D**. The capital-investment measure may not equal cash paid for additions to property, plant and equipment in the same year, but the order of magnitude is unmistakable. TOK is spending heavily to expand.

This is potentially attractive because once a chemical plant is qualified and reaches high utilization, additional throughput can spread fixed costs across a larger revenue base. I would like to see earnings and operating cash flow rise faster than the need for replacement and incremental growth investments.

I will not assume that outcome. Advanced materials require testing, cleanrooms, new formulas, regional supply reliability and sometimes new factories for every generation. Geographic duplication may improve resilience but lower asset utilization. If TOK must keep spending at the present elevated rate simply to maintain share and quality, future earnings growth will not translate into the cash flow I need.

I am buying a possibility of improved **capital conversion**. I am not recording that improvement as an accomplished fact.

## The valuation is the weakest part of the purchase

TOK's last completed Tokyo trading session before this decision was **October 21, 2026**, when the stock closed at **¥8,291**, up **4.82%** on the day. The stock had fallen substantially from its earlier 2026 high, but the price decline by itself is not a margin of safety. Part of the subsequent bounce followed material news that investors can already see.

TOK's July/August full-year FY2026 guidance was **¥291.0 billion revenue, ¥60.6 billion operating income and ¥355.21 diluted/forecast EPS**. At the ¥8,291 reference, the price is about **23.3 times** that forecast EPS. This is not an implausible multiple for a high-quality growing materials business, but it is not obviously cheap.

The more serious reservation comes from the full discounted cash flow work archived in my separate TOK research. A **dated, unreconciled analyst snapshot** used approximately **¥466.17 billion 2030 sales, ¥108.83 billion operating profit and ¥68.60 billion reported free cash flow**. In that research, a 9% WACC and 2% terminal-growth approximation produced about **¥854.4 billion enterprise value**. At the older ¥7,910 reference price the estimated market EV was about **¥920 billion**. Updating only the share price to ¥8,291 moves the same approximate market EV to about **¥966 billion**, before refreshing net debt or minority interests.

This suggests **the model does not offer a clean discount at the latest pre-trade reference price**. It is sensitive to the discount rate: at 8.5% WACC the earlier model produced approximately ¥924.6 billion EV. More importantly, the data vendor's "free cash flow" has not yet been fully reconciled into consistent **unlevered FCFF attributable to the enterprise**, including working capital, cash capex and the economic treatment of minority interests. It would be dishonest to turn either output into a precise price target.

A useful sensitivity check: under the simplified 9%/2% valuation, bridging the roughly **¥111 billion gap** between ¥854 billion modeled EV and approximately ¥966 billion market EV using only a higher sustainable terminal-period FCFF would require on the order of **¥11 billion additional annual FCFF starting around 2030**, holding the rest of the DCF constant. That is **not** a forecast and is **not** automatically €7,500 of attractive expected alpha. It shows how large the cash-flow improvement would have to be to justify the current price under this particular set of assumptions. To earn a meaningful excess return, TOK would generally need to do **better than merely bridge the gap**, or justify a lower required return through lower risk.

This is why I am willing to make a meaningful but not top-sized purchase. My conviction in the business's competitive potential is stronger than my conviction in a large current valuation discount.

## What needs to happen for this to be a good investment

My preferred path does **not** require every cooperative announcement to create a new project. I need a narrower combination of measurable developments.

First, TOK needs to sustain or gain qualifications in valuable EUV and advanced-packaging processes as chipmakers introduce new nodes. A stronger product mix matters more than gross wafer volume alone.

Second, the new Koriyama and Korean capacity needs to become productive without causing a permanently higher ratio of capital spending to sales. Higher depreciation is acceptable if the resulting operating returns comfortably exceed the cost of the invested capital.

Third, there must be evidence that new packaging materials are becoming a material contributor to TOK's total earnings rather than delivering impressive percentage growth from a tiny base.

Fourth, any value from US-JOINT, AIMECHATEC or MTR must translate into observable series-production wins, revenue and margins. Membership, an MOU or a customer's evaluation is one step short of this outcome.

Finally, the share price must not already capitalize all of those gains. If forecasts rise but the valuation multiple compresses faster, I can still lose money or underperform the S&P 500.

## Risk: what can actually go wrong

I would put the **business moat above average**, but the **stock at medium-to-high investment risk** over a one-to-three-year period. The risks are not all the same:

**Technological substitution — high-consequence, real risk.** TOK's historical customers may use new metal-oxide and dry-resist technologies in their future process architectures. JSR/Inpria, Lam and other established competitors have real capital, patents and customer access. The response through internal MOR/SMR and the MTR partnership is credible, but unproven in volume production.

**Cyclicality and concentration — material.** TOK's revenue depends on semiconductor utilization, advanced logic, memory, HBM investment and customer fabrication plans. Spending booms can create overcapacity, and a few large manufacturing customers can materially affect growth or product qualifications.

**Capital efficiency — perhaps the largest investment-specific risk.** The new facilities absorb cash before producing their expected benefit. If repeated technology changes require recurring expansion capex and R&D, EBIT growth will overstate equity-owner wealth creation.

**Valuation — material.** The market already expects substantial growth. The earlier 9% DCF has no clear margin of safety at the October 21 close. Higher growth by itself is not evidence that the stock is cheap.

**Geopolitics and regulation — mixed, not purely beneficial.** Japan-US-Korea-EU coordination can support local supply chains; export controls, onshoring mandates, China restrictions and second-source policies may impose costs and benefit competitors. TOK's American or allied customers do not grant it a permanent right to supply them.

**FX and geographic risk — material.** TOK reports in JPY, sells internationally and manufactures in Japan and other markets. My allocation is recorded in EUR. The return against a USD S&P 500 benchmark therefore reflects the JPY stock price, JPY/EUR and EUR/USD currency paths, dividends, tax assumptions and consistent benchmark timing. Yen strength may help my euro translation while affecting TOK's local-currency reported operations differently; there is no one-way currency benefit. Earthquakes, energy costs and supply interruptions add manufacturing risk.

**Portfolio overlap — material.** This is a new *business model* exposure relative to software and cloud names, but not an independent bet on the global economy. I already own several companies whose results depend directly or indirectly on AI spending and semiconductor investment. TOK could decline along with them during an AI infrastructure slowdown.

The distribution of outcomes is asymmetric only **if** the cash-flow conversion is better than what the stock already discounts. I do not regard it as an inherently low-risk, guaranteed-alpha position.

## Return objective, benchmark and attribution

The purpose of this position is to improve the expected **long-run risk-adjusted outcome** of the portfolio and, if my company-specific expectation-gap thesis is correct, to contribute to outperformance against the S&P 500. It is **not** a prediction of a specific short-term percentage gain, nor a claim that a 2026 stock rebound constitutes alpha.

A USD-denominated S&P 500 comparison must align both the **date and currency** of each capital flow. If I treat TOK as a JPY-denominated security purchased with EUR portfolio capital, I must value the purchase with an explicitly chosen EUR/JPY reference and translate performance using consistent dates and FX. The benchmark must be funded at the same effective time and measured on the same currency basis. Dividends, splits, charges and any later position change must be recorded. Capital-time normalization is a useful supplemental statistic, but it is not by itself identical to a fully time-weighted portfolio or risk-adjusted factor-alpha calculation.

The previous portfolio's reported 10-plus-percentage-point capital-time outperformance over a short period is a historical reconstruction with unresolved pricing and cash-flow assumptions, not an independently audited estimate of persistent annual alpha. I will not use it to claim that this purchase has an established statistical edge.

I will also keep **business-outcome tests** separate from **market-return tests**. TOK can fulfill the manufacturing thesis and still underperform if the stock price already discounts the result. The strongest evidence of investment skill would be a documented, genuinely unexpected operating development that later translates into excess return at the actual recorded entry price.

## The scenarios I am underwriting

**Failure case.** Alternative resist technologies win important process steps, the expansion program is more capital-intensive than expected, or the AI semiconductor supply chain enters a downcycle. TOK's margins, cash conversion or market share disappoint. The multiple contracts and the shares can fall substantially despite a surviving business. A 25–40% stock decline over a bad semiconductor/valuation cycle is plausible as a *stress case*, not a predicted drawdown. I must be prepared to reassess the investment on evidence rather than average down reflexively.

**Middle case.** TOK continues to supply key processes and grows with the industry. WHS expands, new factories ramp, but cash flows broadly match what analysts already expect. The stock may deliver a reasonable return from growth and dividends, yet little or no excess return compared with the S&P 500. In the existing 9% DCF this case could still be unattractive at the observed price.

**Success case.** TOK gains more high-value EUV and packaging process slots than consensus expects. An increasing number of qualified materials are used per advanced chip and customers adopt newly developed technologies. Koriyama and Pyeongtaek move from consuming capital to producing attractive incremental returns. Free cash flow after all relevant investments and minority claims rises above the baseline expectation, and the market revises its cash-flow outlook before the share price fully reflects the improvement.

I cannot assign objective probabilities to these scenarios from the evidence available today. Pretending otherwise would make the thesis look more scientific than it is.

## What would make me change the position

I intend to judge TOK primarily on operating evidence and revised valuation, not a price-based stop-loss alone.

**I would reduce or exit** if a new-node loss undermines the core material franchise, if repeated investment cycles destroy the prospective incremental returns, if the newest partnerships fail to result in relevant commercial qualifications, or if the valuation becomes impossible to justify after updating the cash-flow path. I would also review exposure if TOK and my other holdings become too dependent on the same AI-capex factor.

**I would consider increasing exposure only after a separate new decision record** demonstrates both better company-specific evidence and an attractive *incremental* expected return at the then-current price. This document authorizes and records **no future purchase** beyond the executed €7,500. Neither a share-price drop nor a vague bullish partnership press release is sufficient by itself.

**I would continue holding through volatility** if material qualifications, profitable factory utilization and the cash-flow trajectory remain intact and the expected return remains satisfactory. A share-price rise alone does not confirm the thesis; a fall alone does not disprove it.
