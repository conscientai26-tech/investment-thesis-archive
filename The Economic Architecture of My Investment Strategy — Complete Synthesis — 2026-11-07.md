# The Economic Architecture of My Investment Strategy

### Technology, industrial structure, capital, corporate expansion, valuation and portfolio construction

**7 November 2026**

This document brings together the investment strategies I have developed and recorded since July 2026. It is a synthesis of my research, not a claim that every hypothesis has already been proved. Some ideas have developed into concrete company analyses and dated portfolio decisions. Others remain economic models awaiting a convincing empirical test. I distinguish the two deliberately.

The underlying philosophy and the methodology for evaluating returns are documented separately in [The Architecture of Alpha](./The%20Architecture%20of%20Alpha%20%E2%80%94%20An%20Investment%20Philosophy%20%E2%80%94%202026-11-07.md), [The Root Moat, Reinvestment, and Expectations Engine](./The%20Root%20Moat%20Reinvestment%20and%20Expectations%20Engine%20%E2%80%94%202026-11-07.md), and the [August 2026 methodology update](./Investment%20Strategy%20%26%20Track%20Record%20Methodology%20Update%20%E2%80%94%202026-08-09). Those documents remain part of the record. The present work describes the actual economic strategies in one place.

## I. How technological change redistributes economic power

### 1. Scarcity migration and the bottleneck cycle

My starting point is that a breakthrough rarely increases the value of every participant in its industry equally. When a capability becomes cheaper, complementary resources can become more important. Artificial intelligence may reduce the cost of writing software, preparing analyses and generating technical designs. The ability to deploy that output safely, connect it to real information, power the necessary infrastructure and turn it into useful work can then become relatively scarce.

I study the production system as a sequence of constraints. Additional GPUs have limited value without suitable memory, networking or electricity. An abundance of generated software creates additional demand for testing, security, integration and maintenance. A flood of information increases the economic importance of reliable sources, context and decisions. When investment removes one constraint, the binding constraint can move elsewhere. My task is to anticipate that movement before its economic consequences are reflected in securities prices.

A bottleneck is not, by itself, an attractive stock. Its owner must possess some ability to retain the economic value it enables. I therefore examine substitution, capacity expansion, customer bargaining power, competition, regulation and the capital needed to earn the projected returns.

### 2. Scarce-core expansion

Certain businesses already possess assets that technological progress makes more useful. Such assets include trusted transaction networks, unique data, rights, institutional relationships, embedded operating processes, authoritative system states and distribution channels. If general intelligence becomes cheap, access to these harder-to-reproduce resources can become more valuable.

This approach favors businesses whose existing advantage is strengthened by technological change. A company need not invent an entirely new market to benefit. It may be able to serve more transactions, handle more complicated tasks or enter adjacent activities using resources it already controls. In my research, SAP, RELX, Wolters Kluwer, the London Stock Exchange Group and other infrastructure-like businesses illustrate different versions of this possibility.

The distinction between advantage expansion and advantage migration matters. Expansion builds on an operating strength already demonstrated. Migration requires that a company carry its advantage into a new environment. Both can work, but the latter usually depends on more unproved assumptions.

### 3. Catch-up giants

An incumbent can fall behind a technological leader without losing its ability to compete. Established companies may retain capital, customers, distribution, engineering resources and infrastructure that allow them to close a technological gap. Their potential can be underestimated when investors identify leadership exclusively with the first company to commercialize an innovation.

The attractive case is a business whose existing assets make catching up cheaper and faster than an outsider would expect. Strong demand can make this more valuable if the leader cannot supply the entire market. My investigations of Oracle, Samsung, SAP, Salesforce and large cloud platforms explore different forms of this argument.

I do not infer success merely from corporate size. Catching up must be technically plausible and economically rewarding. Capital, installed customers and distribution are useful only if the firm can convert them into competitive products and durable cash flow.

### 4. Bottleneck asymmetry

Some products represent a small part of their customers' expenditure yet control a much larger share of the outcome. A minor failure in semiconductor design software can affect a project worth billions. A relatively inexpensive component of an industrial system may determine reliability, certification or production yield.

The investment opportunity arises when the supplier can charge for the value of avoiding a large loss rather than merely for the cost of producing its own product. Synopsys, Cadence, specialized chipmaking equipment, verification software and selected industrial components fit this line of investigation.

A high system-wide shadow value does not ensure pricing power. Customers may have substitutes or force savings back into contract prices. I look for evidence that the provider has historically captured a meaningful part of the value created and that the advantage remains difficult to remove.

### 5. Bottlenecks beyond the vertical-integration frontier

Large customers have strong incentives to internalize expensive activities. Hyperscalers design chips, develop software and build infrastructure because dependence on outside vendors can be costly. The investment question is which external suppliers remain difficult to replace even when their customers are extraordinarily well financed.

High-end semiconductor manufacturing, specialized equipment, precision materials, advanced process control and parts of electrical infrastructure are candidates. Their defenses may involve decades of accumulated engineering, unusually high yield requirements, specialized suppliers, certification and irreducible physical construction time.

I prefer to understand why a buyer cannot reproduce a capability on acceptable terms. Merely observing that a supplier is indispensable today tells me too little about the next decade.

### 6. Anticipating the next bottleneck

A bottleneck strategy becomes more useful when applied dynamically. I try to map the transition from one constrained resource to the next: compute, high-bandwidth memory, packaging, networking, optical communication, cooling, energy generation, grid connections and industrial installation capacity.

These are not necessarily successive stages on a fixed timetable. Several constraints can bind at once. Technical improvements can remove one faster than anticipated. The purpose of the map is to identify companies whose earnings may improve when the market begins to recognize a constraint that was previously peripheral.

I distinguish the appearance of an operational constraint from the point at which customers can obtain additional supply. The gap between those dates helps determine whether scarcity can persist long enough to support attractive economics.

### 7. Industrialized enterprise AI deployment

Developing a model and converting it into a dependable business process are different achievements. Large enterprises have legacy systems, complicated permissions, incomplete data, regulatory requirements and processes that cannot simply be replaced by a chat interface.

I expect opportunities to arise in the industrialization of deployment: software that discovers useful applications, understands existing workflows, builds integrations, controls execution, checks outcomes and repeatedly improves production systems. ServiceNow, SAP, Salesforce, UiPath and Pegasystems have appeared in this research because they operate near real organizational work.

The investment case requires more than demonstrations. I look for production usage, measurable customer savings, paid workloads, contract expansion and evidence that the supplier's own economics improve. More deployments are valuable only if they produce more sustainable earnings.

### 8. Friction intelligence and institutional learning

A successful deployment generates information unavailable from a laboratory benchmark. It shows which workflows fail, which exceptions recur, where people intervene and which improvements actually work. Over time, that experience can form an asset.

I refer to this as friction intelligence. An organization that systematically records failed integrations, production incidents, corrective actions and operating outcomes may become better at the next deployment. The advantage is stronger when its knowledge is specific, difficult to reproduce and connected to proprietary processes.

I do not treat the mere possession of logs as a moat. Information must improve future decisions, and the company must have lawful and practical access to use it. An enterprise software provider may help each customer build its own process memory without owning a common pool of all customers' data.

### 9. Ordering infrastructure

Artificial intelligence may make information production abundant while making reliable information organization more difficult. Organizations need persistent records of what happened, what was claimed, what changed, who is authorized to act and how an observation affects an existing decision.

Ordering infrastructure is the layer between raw information and coordinated action. It includes verified states, context, provenance, event records, persistent memory, semantic relationships, permissions and executable workflows. Microsoft, Palantir, ServiceNow, financial-data providers and enterprise systems illustrate different positions in this stack.

The investment advantage would arise when these systems become embedded in operational decisions and retain assets that alternative model providers cannot reconstruct cheaply. The risk is that general platforms absorb the functionality, or that the infrastructure remains useful without producing enough profit for its owner.

### 10. Specialized AI alongside general models

I do not assume that one universal model will capture the economics of every industry. General models can be used as inputs by companies that possess specific data, experimental procedures, engineering tools, customer access and the ability to verify results in the real world.

Specialized AI may prove economically attractive in science, engineering, healthcare administration, regulated services and other fields where the difficult part of a task is not generating plausible text. The durable value could lie in the complete, verified process rather than the specialized model itself.

The difficult question is whether a general-model company could integrate the specialty at low cost. I investigate which capabilities rely on temporary model limitations and which rely on durable external resources. The latter are more defensible foundations for an investment.

### 11. Interconnected AI and the distributed personal computer

I have examined a future in which intelligence is distributed across phones, wearables, earbuds, glasses, vehicles, connected appliances and eventually robots. These devices could share context and divide work between local processors and cloud systems.

The economic opportunity reaches beyond the model provider. Low-power semiconductors, wireless connectivity, sensors, device operating systems, permissions and personal computing platforms become important. Qualcomm, Ambiq, Alphabet, Xiaomi and the broader consumer-device ecosystem have featured in this research.

I distinguish the number of connected devices from their economic usefulness. An attractive supplier must control a component that becomes more valuable with widespread deployment and can preserve its position as chip efficiency, product designs and integration methods change.

### 12. Personal AI orchestration

Persistent personal AI could become a coordination layer across applications and devices. Such a system would retain useful context, understand user permissions, route tasks to appropriate tools and maintain continuity as the user moves between services.

The possible advantage comes from trusted integration into a person's actual digital environment. Access to accounts, calendars, applications and history may be more difficult to reproduce than the language model used to interpret them. This hypothesis is connected to my work on Conscient, which has provided practical experience with contextual assistance and the difficulty of reliable deployment.

This is a commercial hypothesis, not evidence that a standalone personal AI platform will succeed. Operating systems and existing application providers may internalize much of the functionality. I therefore pay particular attention to permission control, distribution, switching costs and whether any independent platform can retain economic value.

### 13. Physical AI and industrial robotics

More capable AI can affect the physical economy through robots, machine tools, inspection systems, industrial controllers, sensors and automated logistics. A useful analysis begins with the full production process rather than with a demonstration of a humanoid robot.

Japan's robotics and precision-equipment industry is an important area of my research. FANUC and related industrial suppliers offer exposure to automation without requiring one specific consumer-facing robot design to win. Semiconductor manufacturing, electrical infrastructure and machine-building may also gain from the expansion.

The main questions are adoption economics, reliability, integration costs, installed-base advantages and replacement cycles. Robot installations can rise while individual manufacturers experience falling margins, so volume growth must be connected to owner earnings.

## II. Capital, expansion and the economic structure of firms

### 14. AI ecosystem compounding

A business with several related activities may possess more economic power than a similar collection of unrelated assets. Cloud infrastructure can support model development; models can improve products; products can generate demand for cloud capacity; distribution can attract developers and enterprises.

I am interested in this reciprocal use of resources. Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta provide different cases. AI may reduce internal coordination and development costs, allowing business units to create useful complementary demand for one another.

The correct test is incremental economic output. A conglomerate does not become attractive merely because its divisions share a corporate parent. Cross-business integration must produce measurable efficiency, customer retention, product expansion or additional cash flow.

### 15. Strategic orphan options

A small subsidiary or undeveloped capability inside a large company can have substantial future potential while contributing little to current valuation. This can be missed when analysts concentrate almost entirely on the dominant business.

My research has considered Sony's sports-technology activities, including Hawk-Eye and Pulselive, as an example of a potentially interesting option inside a much larger group. The relevant question is whether the parent can turn a narrow capability into a broader platform using capital, distribution, data and related relationships.

I calculate the possible contribution relative to the parent company's total earnings and valuation. A tenfold increase in a tiny subsidiary may still be immaterial. The option matters only when its scale, probability and economic terms could change shareholder value.

### 16. Reusable expansion engines

Certain companies can enter new services using assets already assembled for their original business. A payment network can use merchant relationships, transaction infrastructure and trusted identity systems to provide additional data, security and commercial services.

Mastercard is a prominent case in my research. I have studied the possibility that its network serves as a door into adjacent profit pools and that each successful extension reduces the cost of subsequent extensions.

The risk is double counting. Existing distribution and customer relationships may enable several products, but revenue forecasts must not assume that the same underlying economic value can be collected repeatedly. I test each new service for customer willingness to pay, genuine incremental costs and defensible profit.

### 17. Capital conversion and the three states of investment

Revenue growth is not enough. Shareholder value depends on how much capital is required to generate that revenue and how much cash remains after the business maintains and expands itself.

I separate the realized base, capital already committed but still at risk, and the future capital frontier. The realized base contains demonstrated operations and earnings. Capital at risk includes projects whose returns are not yet known. The future frontier describes what must still be spent to achieve management's plans.

Incremental return on invested capital matters especially for capital-intensive businesses. A company's historical return may be high while its next investment earns very little. Conversely, a temporary decline in reported free cash flow may be rational if new investments have attractive future returns. I examine project cohorts, operating margins, capital intensity, time to utilization, depreciation, financing and cash flow per share.

### 18. AI capital expenditure as strategic optionality

Major AI capital investment should not be judged solely by the revenue directly attributable to a new facility. Capacity can provide options: faster product development, lower dependence on suppliers, access to future demand and the ability to serve complementary business units.

My Microsoft and Amazon analyses have examined the possibility that investment now can protect a future competitive position even if near-term free cash flow looks unattractive. A decision to refrain from investing also has an opportunity cost when competitors can use the infrastructure to improve their capabilities.

I do not treat this as permission to disregard investment discipline. An option has value only if its plausible payoffs justify the capital committed. The analysis must consider utilization, obsolescence, alternative uses, competitors' response and the possibility that added capacity drives down prices.

### 19. Capital scarcity inside the AI expansion

A rapid expansion of computing, electricity, grid capacity and semiconductor production can make finance itself a constraint. Even technically useful projects may become uneconomic when borrowing costs, collateral requirements or access to equity capital deteriorate.

I documented this hypothesis in August 2026 and later compared it with public comments about financing constraints in AI infrastructure. The important analytical distinction is between demand for the finished service and the ability to finance enough capacity to serve it.

The beneficiaries may be well-capitalized incumbents, sellers of scarce assets, or firms whose business models require relatively little additional capital. The risks fall more heavily on firms dependent on continuous refinancing or optimistic capital-market conditions.

### 20. Compute cycles, oversupply and price formation

A shortage of computing resources can encourage extensive investment. If enough capacity arrives after demand growth slows, scarcity rents can disappear. The same logic applies to memory and other cyclical components.

I distinguish lower unit costs caused by technical progress from falling prices caused by excess supply and competition. The first can expand demand and improve productivity. The second can destroy producer margins even while customers benefit.

I compare announced projects with credible financing, delivery schedules, utilization rates, customer commitments and replacement cycles. The central issue is not whether computing demand grows, but whether additional demand exceeds the growth of economically available supply at prices that justify new investment.

## III. Sector and industry applications

### 21. Electricity, grid access and time to power

AI infrastructure, industrial automation, electrification and manufacturing expansion all depend on sufficient electrical power. The binding resource is frequently more specific than electricity in the abstract. It may be a grid connection at the right location, a transformer, an approved site or a transmission upgrade that can be completed in time.

My research has included Vistra, Eaton, Quanta Services, utilities and related infrastructure suppliers. The opportunity can sit with energy producers, equipment manufacturers, engineering contractors or owners of usable sites, depending on which constraint actually binds.

I evaluate lead times, regulation, project economics and local demand. Announced data-center capacity is not equivalent to approved, connected and operating capacity. A company with access to usable power sooner than competitors may control an economically meaningful advantage.

### 22. Semiconductor materials and manufacturing yield

Advanced semiconductor production relies on specialized chemicals, photoresists and process materials. Their direct cost can be small relative to the output they help produce, while failure or poor yield can be exceptionally expensive.

Tokyo Ohka Kogyo has featured in this research because of its exposure to difficult-to-replace photochemical processes. My thesis is not that every material supplier benefits proportionally from AI demand. I examine process qualification, supplier concentration, customer dependence, pricing, yield requirements and capital intensity.

The economic advantage has to survive process transitions. A technically crucial material can become less valuable if a different manufacturing approach reduces its use or a customer successfully qualifies an alternative.

### 23. Memory, advanced packaging and edge computing

Increasing AI capability can raise demand for memory bandwidth, packaging, interconnects and local processing. In some applications, moving data is more expensive than performing the arithmetic itself. The architecture of the system determines which component becomes scarce.

My research on Micron, memory producers, Qualcomm and Ambiq considers separate sources of demand: high-bandwidth data-center memory, conventional memory cycles and low-power edge processing. These should not be treated as a single trade.

The main analytical challenge is identifying the relationship between structural demand and industry supply cycles. A producer can have excellent long-term demand prospects but still deliver weak shareholder returns when supply expands too quickly or the purchase valuation assumes unusually high margins indefinitely.

### 24. Cybersecurity and verification as a cost of autonomy

More autonomous systems create more surfaces that require verification, identity, permissions, auditability and protection. As AI moves from generating recommendations to executing actions, the cost of a security failure may rise sharply.

I have examined Palo Alto Networks and other security providers in this context. The attractive economic position would be a trusted security layer whose usage increases with the amount of automated activity, rather than a vendor selling a narrow feature likely to be bundled into a general platform.

I look for evidence of durable customer integration, differentiated threat information, reliable controls and improving free cash flow. The security problem can grow while the profits of individual vendors remain exposed to competition.

### 25. Financial rails, authoritative states and machine transactions

Autonomous economic activity needs more than an intelligent agent. Transactions require trustworthy identities, permissions, settlement, records, contracts and the ability to establish which party owns what. Payment and market infrastructure may therefore remain strategically important as AI improves.

Mastercard, LSEG, ICE and CME have appeared in my research for different reasons. They possess networks, transaction relationships, market data or authoritative financial records that cannot be recreated by text generation alone.

I distinguish transaction growth from the economics earned by the infrastructure provider. An increase in activity can benefit customers or competing intermediaries rather than shareholders. The strongest case combines growing economic relevance with defensible pricing and low marginal capital requirements.

### 26. AI catch-up in established industries

Some opportunities lie outside the AI suppliers. Established businesses can improve their economics by adopting new technology more effectively than the market expects. Insurance administration, legal services, healthcare operations, industrial services and other process-intensive activities may experience changes in cost structures.

The investment is attractive when a company has an incumbent advantage, a credible route to implementation and a valuation that does not already assume successful transformation. A late adopter may benefit if competitors lack the ability to execute the same change.

I separate genuine productivity gains from cosmetic announcements. Lower expenses must be sustainable, implementation costs must be included, and competitors must not force all the benefit into lower customer prices.

## IV. International industrial organization and macroeconomics

### 27. The Allied Scaling Network

I have investigated whether the United States and trusted partner countries could behave increasingly like a coordinated industrial scaling system. Such an arrangement would connect American models, capital, cloud platforms and distribution with the specialized manufacturing, energy, materials, security and engineering capabilities of other countries.

The hypothesis is broader than a political alliance. It concerns the economic effect of reducing coordination friction across complementary industrial systems. Japan's robotics and precision industry, the Netherlands' semiconductor-equipment concentration, South Korean memory manufacturing and other national capabilities may form useful specialized nodes.

I look for companies whose reachable markets, utilization, scale or financing improve because cross-border cooperation becomes easier. This remains a scenario requiring political and institutional evidence, not a settled description of the future.

### 28. Trusted-network globalization

Neither unrestricted global outsourcing nor complete domestic self-sufficiency is necessarily economically optimal. Critical industries may instead become organized around trusted production networks, combining specialization with redundancy.

The investment implications are complicated. A company may benefit from privileged access to a trusted supply chain, but protection can also create inefficiencies and foster additional competition. Export restrictions may raise the value of strategically accessible supply while reducing a firm's total addressable market.

I distinguish global demand from demand a company can legally and practically serve. An industrial asset should be valued according to its role in the accessible economic network, not solely according to the size of the world market.

### 29. Macroeconomic transmission

Macro analysis is useful only when an observed development is connected to the actual economics of a business. An interest-rate change, energy shock or fiscal decision can affect different companies through different channels: financing, consumer demand, margins, capital expenditure, inventory, currency and valuation.

I trace the sequence from event to transmission channel, company economics, expectations and securities prices. I also examine the market's existing expectations because a widely anticipated event may have little remaining effect.

This is an analytical discipline rather than a claim to possess a tested macro-trading strategy. I use it to understand opportunities, portfolio exposures and conditions under which existing investment theses could fail.

### 30. Culture, attention and creative economics

Technology is not the sole driver of long-term value. Culture, entertainment and attention form a separate economic field. Netflix has been an important example in my research because content libraries require continuous reinvestment while audience preferences and competitors change.

The relevant moat may involve distribution, consumer habits, data, brand, content development and the ability to repeatedly produce material people want to watch. Unlike a scarce industrial process, cultural value is difficult to preserve through technical barriers alone.

I analyze the recurring cost of replacing successful content, customer retention, competition for attention and the relationship between spending and durable owner earnings.

### 31. Predictability over complexity

An elaborate business is not necessarily easier to invest in than a simpler one. My Sony and Nintendo comparisons have reinforced the value of forecasting what can be understood with reasonable confidence.

I will leave an apparent opportunity untouched when too many unrelated outcomes determine its valuation. A business may possess numerous assets and attractive optionality but still be difficult to value if the important sources of future profits are uncertain.

This is a constraint on research confidence, not an assumption that simple companies always offer higher returns. Complexity may create mispricing, but only when I can assess it better than the market.

## V. Expectations, market selection and the research process

### 32. The expectations gap

An excellent business can be a poor investment at a price that assumes extraordinary future success. Conversely, an imperfect company can generate strong returns when reality exceeds depressed expectations.

I compare the future economics implied by the current valuation with the outcomes I consider plausible. Reverse valuation is useful because it forces the analyst to ask what must happen for the quoted price to be justified before constructing an optimistic forecast.

I seek investments where the difference between market expectations and realistic business outcomes is both material and supported by an identifiable causal advantage. For the active portfolio, the expected result must also compare favorably with the index and the best available alternatives.

### 33. Expectation resets after sharp price declines

A large decline can create an attractive opportunity, but price action alone is not evidence of undervaluation. I investigate which expectation changed, whether the underlying business was genuinely damaged and whether the new price adequately reflects remaining risks.

A decline caused by temporary uncertainty may differ from one caused by permanent deterioration in demand, margins or capital returns. A company's recovery potential must be evaluated against what investors now believe, not against its former peak price.

I retain the ability to reverse a decision after a major new disclosure. Previous ownership, an old target price or the desire to recover a loss does not create an investment case.

### 34. Staged accumulation through expectation cycles

I can establish a modest initial position when the thesis is promising but incomplete, then increase exposure as valuation improves or evidence strengthens. A lower price may justify adding if the fundamental outlook remains intact and the expected return has improved.

This is not automatic averaging down. The new purchase must be judged independently. If the event causing the decline also undermines the thesis, buying more merely because the stock is cheaper would increase exposure to a deteriorating investment.

The advantage of staged accumulation is that it lets capital allocation respond to both information and price. The disadvantage is the possibility of underweighting a good opportunity or repeatedly adding to a mistaken one.

### 35. Portfolio rotation and alpha decay

An investment's remaining attractiveness changes as prices rise, fundamentals develop and expectations adjust. I want to measure the amount of prospective alpha left in a holding rather than treating the original thesis as a permanent reason to own it.

Over longer periods, I intend to compare successive groups of investments and determine whether reallocating capital toward new expectation gaps improves results. This includes a five-year research program on thesis rotation and alpha decay.

The test must consider taxes where relevant, transaction costs, spreads, risk, timing mistakes and the performance of a simpler holding strategy. Rotation is useful only if it improves the total economic result.

### 36. The portfolio as a map of economic ecosystems

Conventional sector classifications can conceal shared economic dependencies. Several companies listed in different sectors may depend on the same AI capital expenditure cycle, the same consumer advertising market or the same interest-rate assumption.

I map exposures by economic ecosystem and by thesis. Compute, enterprise deployment, electricity, physical AI, financial rails, consumer demand and industrial expansion are examples. I examine both observable market correlations and hidden correlations arising from common causal assumptions.

Performance should be decomposed into market, sector, ecosystem and security-selection effects. Outperforming an index during a favorable industry cycle does not prove that individual companies were selected unusually well.

### 37. Testing universal predictive signals

I want to find repeatable variables that precede excess returns rather than relying on memorable stock examples. Candidate signals include valuation hurdles, market neglect, analyst consensus, earnings expectations, capital conversion and the durability of competitive advantages.

One recently explored hypothesis concerns overlooked companies with unusually low thresholds for positive surprises and strong agreement among analysts. The idea requires historical testing before it can be treated as a reliable strategy.

Any backtest must use information available at the original decision date, include unsuccessful candidates, avoid survivorship and look-ahead bias, and report the distribution of outcomes rather than only an attractive average. Transaction costs, benchmark selection and changing market regimes matter.

### 38. The technology and opportunity radar

I monitor scientific and engineering developments before restricting attention to listed companies. The research sequence begins with a technological change, identifies practical applications, follows the necessary production system, considers which firms control essential resources and finally examines their securities prices.

This approach allows the research universe to change when the economy changes. It also reduces the temptation to force every news item into an existing holding.

A useful radar records dates, original sources, alternative explanations and explicit conditions that would change the interpretation. Technological novelty is only the start of the investigation.

## VI. Additional models developed from the same research

### 39. AI abundance and the economics of deflation

If cognitive production costs fall sharply, the price of many individual services may fall even as the volume consumed expands. Corporate profit depends on price, cost and quantity. A lower unit price does not prevent higher aggregate earnings when costs fall further or the market expands substantially.

I am particularly interested in the different supply elasticities of economic inputs. Reproducible digital services may become cheaper while grid access, sites, rights, unique data, networks and other scarce assets rise in relative value. Technological abundance can therefore coexist with concentrated economic rents.

The longer-run hypothesis includes demand. An economy capable of producing far more goods and services still requires consumers and institutions able to pay for them. If productive capacity and purchasing power diverge, effective demand can itself become a constraint. I treat this as a possible economic regime, not a certain forecast.

### 40. Generalized deployment machines

A corporation may eventually compete through its ability to identify economic constraints, allocate capital, create productive capacity, observe results and redeploy resources faster than competitors. The competence becomes broader than making one product well.

I call this a generalized deployment machine. Its strength would come from combining analytical capability, capital, distribution, physical assets and real-world learning in a repeatable process. AI could reduce the cost of discovery, engineering and internal reorganization, making additional business lines more economical to establish.

The investment case is strongest when the resulting businesses reinforce one another. A large balance sheet with poor capital allocation is not enough. I look for evidence that repeated deployment improves returns and increases the value of the existing economic core.

### 41. Enterprise elasticity

A company can adopt more powerful models without becoming much more productive if its internal processes remain difficult to change. Enterprise elasticity measures the ability to understand, redesign, approve, deploy and improve real organizational work.

My Pegasystems research develops this idea through complex case management, decision rules, permissions, regulatory logic, process variations and historical operating states. Institutional intent can remain scarce even when the code implementing it becomes cheap to generate.

An attractive provider should make difficult enterprise processes more adaptable while retaining control of a durable economic asset. Customer value is not enough. The provider must translate deployment success into recurring revenue, margins and owner cash flow; otherwise the technology is valuable but the stock thesis is weak.

### 42. The transnational American AI stack

American AI platforms may develop additional advantages by operating through trusted international networks. Models, computing infrastructure, finance, enterprise software and distribution can be combined with partners' specialized industrial, security and scientific capabilities.

Deployment across different countries and institutions could generate a wider set of practical lessons, improving products and lowering the cost of subsequent integrations. My work considers Israel's security and cyber capabilities as one possible complementary node, alongside other industrial strengths among American partners.

I treat international cooperation as a conditional economic mechanism. Data rules, national interests, export restrictions and competition can prevent the expected feedback loop. The analysis must establish who controls customer access, rights and profits within the resulting system.

### 43. Coordination-discount compression

Institutional fragmentation can leave existing assets underused. Differing standards, procurement rules, regulations and national boundaries may prevent firms from reaching customers they could otherwise serve efficiently.

Closer coordination can expand the economically accessible market and improve plant utilization without requiring a technological breakthrough. The potential gain comes from lower friction in using resources that already exist.

I study which businesses could receive more orders, improve scale economics and earn higher returns if the coordination cost falls. The existence of a larger addressable market does not prove that the firm can capture it profitably.

### 44. Common procurement and strategic redundancy

Capital-intensive production often depends on confidence in future demand. Coordinated long-term procurement by states or large industrial buyers can make new factories more financeable, support larger production runs and encourage specialized suppliers to expand.

Strategic redundancy creates a related opportunity. A second supplier can become economically valuable even if it is not the lowest-cost producer, because dependence on a single source creates substantial risks for customers.

The investment case requires attention to contract quality, capital intensity, subsidies, customer concentration and eventual competition. A politically important factory can still generate poor returns for its owners.

### 45. Standards and trusted infrastructure

Identity systems, certification, secure communications, technical interfaces and interoperability standards allow complex networks of organizations to work together. Their economic importance may grow as industrial and digital ecosystems become more interconnected.

A firm controlling a trusted operating layer may benefit from many downstream applications without predicting which end product succeeds. Relevant areas include industrial software, verification, financial infrastructure, secure communications and supply-chain identity.

Standards can also be open and competitive. I distinguish the public value of interoperability from the private ability to capture fees, distribution or durable switching costs.

### 46. Resource-light giants

Companies differ in how much additional scarce physical capacity they must provide when demand increases. A data-center operator may need substantial new equipment and electricity. An embedded software, rights or state-control business may be able to scale its economically valuable core with less proportional investment.

I call the ratio between additional demand and required expansion of scarce inputs expansion friction. A compelling business combines low expansion friction with a core that competitors cannot easily copy or bypass.

Palantir is a useful conceptual case because institutional state, decision rights and process integration can be valuable without ownership of the underlying cloud hardware. This remains an empirical question about its actual pricing, retention and competition, not an automatic endorsement of the stock.

### 47. Overflow competition

Rapid market growth can create room for outsiders even when incumbents remain strong. If a leading platform cannot add capacity fast enough, competitors may serve the unfilled demand without initially taking customers away from it.

Specialist AI infrastructure firms such as CoreWeave or Nebius illustrate the potential. The opportunity may be large but temporary. When incumbent capacity catches up, supply becomes less scarce and the outsider can lose pricing power.

I therefore distinguish an overflow window from a durable moat. A successful entrant must convert its temporary access to demand into lasting relationships, infrastructure advantages, differentiated services or another difficult-to-reproduce asset. This has different risk and sizing implications from owning a mature scarce core.

### 48. AGI as a complete working system

My functional definition of AGI concerns a system that can independently identify, define, execute, verify and complete valuable tasks across different fields of work. It need not be one model or exceed the best human expert at every individual activity.

Its essential functions include objective formation, understanding the current situation, recognizing problems, asking useful questions, memory, planning, coordination, tool use, execution, verification and correction. The reliability of the whole process matters more than the eloquence of an isolated model response.

This definition provides an investment map. I can examine which functions become cheap general capabilities, which require specialized integration and which remain tied to irreducible outside resources. I then distinguish technical competence from the ability to preserve competitive advantage and earn economic returns.

### 49. The continuing compute competition after functional AGI

Reaching a useful definition of AGI need not end competition among AI firms. Companies can continue to improve reliability, autonomy, latency, operating costs and the scope of tasks their systems can complete.

That creates an investment rationale for continuing research and hardware development, including custom chips designed by large users. More efficient hardware can lower the cost of useful work, while the prospect of falling behind a rival creates a defensive incentive to invest.

I analyze the entire causal chain before inferring demand for equipment suppliers: additional compute must improve useful capability or economics; that improvement must justify investment; the investment must generate demand for particular components; and the component provider must capture profit. My EDA research on Synopsys and Cadence is connected to this sequence.

### 50. Time to binding versus time to supply response

The durability of a bottleneck depends on timing. A constrained resource may command high prices when demand becomes binding, but competitors can erode those returns if they respond quickly enough.

I compare the date a constraint begins to limit economic output with the time required to add effective supply. This involves engineering lead times, permitting, qualification, financing and customer switching.

A shortage that lasts six months is different from a bottleneck that takes a decade to reproduce. The same distinction helps explain why scarce physical infrastructure, process knowledge and standardized software features may have very different economic value.

### 51. The fundamental clock and the market clock

A business transformation and its market revaluation occur on different schedules. A product may take years to reach meaningful production revenue while investors respond much earlier to credible evidence of its success.

I distinguish the fundamental clock, which concerns adoption, execution and cash flow, from the market clock, which concerns changes in investor beliefs. A stock can rise before the full earnings benefit appears or remain expensive after expectations have already moved far ahead of results.

The relationship affects entry timing, opportunity cost and monitoring. I prefer an explicit schedule of operational milestones and expected evidence rather than a vague belief that the market will eventually recognize a good story.

## VII. Risk, valuation, portfolio decisions and evidence

### Finding a root economic advantage

Before valuing a company, I attempt to find the underlying cause of its economic power. High margins, customer retention and market share may all stem from the same cause. Counting them as three independent strengths creates false confidence.

I trace the root advantage through customer dependence, substitution, bargaining power and the actual ability to maintain returns. I then classify how technological and competitive change could affect it: erosion, failure of a necessary dependency, migration to a new environment or expansion of the existing advantage.

The second step is examining reinvestment. An advantage that earns high returns on its existing capital but cannot reinvest meaningfully may have limited compounding potential. A company with substantial reinvestment opportunities at high incremental returns can be more valuable, provided the acquisition price does not already require that outcome.

### Reverse valuation and a rising purchase hurdle

I first ask what future operating performance the current price implies. Only then do I compare that expectation with my own scenarios. Revenue growth, operating margins, working capital, reinvestment, dilution, taxes and owner cash flow all matter.

My purchase threshold should rise as my research improves. An opportunity that might have qualified early in the development of this approach should not automatically qualify later. Holding a smaller number of carefully researched positions and doing nothing when the advantage is insufficient is preferable to deploying capital for activity's sake.

The relevant hurdle is relative. A company must offer enough expected reward for the uncertainty, downside, correlation and opportunity cost involved. It is not enough for the stock to appear undervalued in isolation.

### Probabilities, alternatives and adverse cases

For each substantial investment I consider a range of outcomes rather than a single target price. A scenario needs an economic mechanism, necessary conditions, evidence and a defensible probability. The downside should include permanent impairment, financing difficulties, weaker margins, adverse competitive responses and the possibility that the market never awards the expected multiple.

I explicitly consider the strongest alternative use of the same capital, including the index or an existing holding. A purchase should survive comparison with that alternative after accounting for concentration, liquidity and the cost of being wrong.

I keep the investment decision separate from my opinion of the company. A compelling business, an attractive stock price and an attractive portfolio addition are three different assessments.

### Post-purchase threat monitoring

Risks should be documented before a purchase, not invented to explain a subsequent loss. I identify major threats, likely consequences, early indicators and conditions under which the thesis would change.

During ownership I monitor the specific risks that mattered at the original decision. A threatening disclosure should be examined through primary information, not through the confidence of television commentators or social-media personalities. I distinguish a temporary market reaction from a genuine deterioration in long-term economics.

Systemic expectation anchors deserve special attention. A widely followed company's earnings, a major financing event or a change in credit conditions can challenge an assumption shared by many holdings. The portfolio can be more concentrated in one economic belief than its number of tickers suggests.

### Buying, trimming, exiting and waiting

No action is a legitimate allocation decision. A position should be maintained because its remaining expected return justifies the risk, not because it was purchased previously or once produced a substantial gain.

When a stock rises quickly I may take partial profits, especially after gains of around 20 percent or more, but the decision should reflect remaining expected alpha, valuation and alternatives rather than a mechanical percentage alone. Material earnings events may also justify reducing exposure when the downside distribution is unusually unfavorable.

Selling does not prohibit reentry. A subsequent purchase requires a fresh assessment of the business, new information and current expectations. Emotional attachment to a previous position has no place in that calculation.

### Measurement, falsification and honest attribution

My research archive began on 18 July 2026. It contains dated theses, virtual investment decisions, revisions, rejected ideas and subsequent reassessments. The formal research methodology introduced a hypothetical one-million-dollar portfolio beginning on 1 September 2026. It is virtual capital, not money personally invested, and its reported returns must remain identified as hypothetical.

The purpose of the archive is to make analysis testable. I want to measure stock selection, allocation, timing, forecast calibration, industry exposure and the influence of luck separately. Equal-weight, immediate-entry and rejected-idea comparison portfolios can help distinguish components of performance. A broad-index comparison should use a consistent total-return benchmark and a method that handles portfolio cash flows correctly.

Original decisions and timestamps should not be rewritten after outcomes are known. Errors, failed theses and uncertainty must remain visible. A sound result is one that survives prospective evaluation across different market conditions, not a retrospective selection of successful examples.

## VIII. Recent research that may become new strategies

The following ideas arose from a review of research and company disclosures published between July and November 2026. They are additions to the research agenda, not established sources of alpha. I have not yet shown that any of them produces superior returns in a historical portfolio test.

### AI-accelerated research compounding

The possibility that AI materially accelerates the research required to build better AI could change the competitive structure of the industry. A company using its existing models to improve future models may enjoy a feedback advantage that is not captured by estimates of training expenditure alone.

I want to distinguish capability improvement per dollar of training from improvement due to algorithmic research, better tools and more productive engineering. An accelerated research loop could make technical advantages more persistent, but it could also hasten the replacement of specialized vendors dependent on current model limitations.

The November 2026 METR assessment of Anthropic's AI-assisted research provides a useful empirical lead, subject to the study's limitations.

### Bottleneck unlocking

A physical constraint may be reduced by better measurement, scheduling or control rather than by building more physical infrastructure. Grid optimization, advanced power-flow management, data-center scheduling and improved factory utilization are possible cases.

I am interested in businesses that can unlock an unusually large amount of customer output with a comparatively small software or engineering intervention. Their products may possess high system-wide value, though the supplier's ability to capture that value still requires proof.

The IEA's September 2026 work on modernization of power grids is an important reference for this approach.

### Financing-adjusted demand

Capital expenditure announcements and large customer contracts can create an impression of independently confirmed demand even when the involved companies finance and buy from one another. Such arrangements may be economically sound, but they can create correlated risks if financing costs rise or ultimate customer demand disappoints.

I intend to trace contractual obligations, equity investments, borrowings, suppliers and customer cash flows through the AI infrastructure ecosystem. The relevant issue is whether demand remains sustainable without repeated injections of capital from the same interconnected participants.

The IMF's October 2026 assessment of the AI ecosystem and financial stability is a useful starting point.

### Autonomous-work unit economics

Token prices are a poor proxy for the cost of a completed autonomous task. Complex systems spend resources on planning, additional model calls, tools, checking, retries and human intervention.

I want to compare the full cost of successfully completed work across providers. A more expensive individual model may still produce cheaper reliable outcomes; an apparently inexpensive system may become costly when failures and repeated attempts are included.

This approach should improve comparisons of agent platforms, specialized AI and enterprise deployment vendors.

### Delegated economic authority

As autonomous systems initiate purchases, payments or other binding actions, they require reliable identity, authorization, transaction limits and verification. Existing payment and institutional infrastructure providers may have advantages in establishing the rules by which agents may act.

I am interested in the economic layer that connects a human or institution's instruction to a securely authorized transaction. Mastercard and Visa initiatives in 2026 suggest that this market is moving from concept toward early commercial experiments.

The investment case depends on whether authority and verification become services that generate durable margins or ordinary features supplied at minimal cost.

### Compute capital productivity

The economic yield from a data center depends on utilization, useful workload output, model efficiency, depreciation and the operating costs of the entire system. The number of installed GPUs alone cannot measure performance.

I want to compare the productive use of existing computing capital and the cash returns generated by incremental infrastructure spending. Technical improvements that release unused capacity can change a company's economics without an equivalent expansion in its capital base.

Microsoft's October 2026 discussion of AI infrastructure and capacity utilization is relevant to this analysis.

### Deployment independence

A software provider may win early projects through intensive, expensive implementation work but fail to build an economically scalable product. Customers may abandon systems that they cannot maintain or adapt without outside engineers.

I want to measure whether deployments become easier and less expensive over time, and whether customers can make routine changes independently while the vendor still controls a defensible core. The result could distinguish durable enterprise platforms from consulting-heavy implementations with fragile margins.

## IX. How I intend to combine the strategies

These hypotheses are connected. I begin with an economic change and trace which constraints it creates or removes. I identify the companies controlling the affected resources, examine how that control can change, and determine whether their economic advantages are likely to endure. I then study what they must invest to exploit the opportunity, how much cash could reach shareholders, and what assumptions the market price already contains.

For a company to qualify, the causal argument must survive adverse questions. Is the apparent bottleneck truly scarce? Can customers bypass or reproduce it? Does the company control the economic rents, or does the value accrue elsewhere? Will additional investment generate attractive returns? What can go wrong before the investment reaches maturity? Which other assets in the portfolio depend on the same assumptions? What would have to happen for the stock to outperform a realistic alternative?

My strongest opportunities should combine an unusual understanding of technological or industrial change with a business whose economic advantages can be explained precisely, a valuation that does not fully anticipate the opportunity and a portfolio allocation that is justified by uncertainty and downside risk.

I do not expect every thesis in this document to survive. Some will be disproved; others will overlap or become obsolete as technology and markets change. The archive exists so that those changes can be studied rather than concealed. The aim is a smaller set of economically sound, testable and repeatable investment decisions built from a much broader body of research.
