## Why I am changing my decision

On August 11 I decided not to invest in Amazon at roughly USD 278 per share.

I still think that decision was reasonable based on the model I had at the time.

The old conclusion was not that Amazon was weak. It was almost the opposite. I thought AWS was exceptional, AI demand was real, Amazon had unusually strong strategic assets and the long-term optionality was large.

The problem was that too much of the upside depended on the least proven part of the thesis.

My rough scenario work at the time suggested that a normal Amazon success case could still produce only ordinary equity returns from a roughly USD 3 trillion starting valuation. The more interesting return profile required something closer to the Generalized Deployment Machine thesis becoming economically real.

At that point I treated that thesis mostly as a distant optionality layer.

I did not think I had enough evidence to pay for it.

I am now opening a USD 3,000 starter position because my model of what has to happen has changed.

The company does not need to suddenly transform into a fully generalized economic machine for the thesis to begin creating value.

There are several smaller mechanisms that can appear earlier, can be tested separately and can reinforce each other.

That makes the opportunity less binary than I originally modeled it.

The stock is also about 6% below the price at which I rejected it, which improves the asymmetry without requiring me to pretend that valuation risk has disappeared.

This is not a reversal from bearish to bullish.

It is a reversal from:

**interesting but too much of the upside depends on a remote unproven state**

to:

**enough of the intermediate mechanisms now look investable to justify a starter position.**

## What changed in my world model

### 1. I no longer think GDM has to arrive as one giant event

My earlier framing was too discrete.

I implicitly treated the larger thesis as though Amazon would either remain AWS + Retail + Advertising or eventually become something fundamentally different.

That is probably not how the transition would happen.

A Generalized Deployment Machine, if it ever emerges, should appear through smaller observable changes first:

- smaller teams producing more output,
- faster internal software and product deployment,
- more workflows handled by agents,
- better reuse of infrastructure across businesses,
- more general robotics,
- faster movement of capital between opportunities,
- lower coordination cost per unit of economic activity,
- and faster resolution of bottlenecks.

This matters for the investment because the market does not need to wait until 2032 for Amazon to announce that it has become a new category of company.

The thesis can begin earning credibility through intermediate evidence.

That gives the prediction a much shorter feedback cycle than I originally assumed.

### 2. Bottleneck resolution may be the more precise Amazon advantage

I originally focused heavily on the assets Amazon owns.

I now think the more useful question is what Amazon can do when one of those assets becomes the next constraint.

Amazon cannot eliminate scarcity.

If it reduces dependence on Nvidia through Trainium, the bottleneck may move to advanced manufacturing, HBM, packaging or power.

If it solves power, the bottleneck may move to construction, networking or permitting.

If it solves those, the bottleneck may eventually move inward to capital allocation and organizational coordination.

The advantage is therefore not "Amazon owns everything."

The more interesting possibility is:

**Amazon can identify, finance and resolve the next bottleneck faster than competitors, while internalizing part of the scarcity rent created around that bottleneck.**

This creates a recursive system.

Better chips can lower compute cost.

Lower compute cost can make internal agents cheaper.

Better agents can improve planning, software development and infrastructure deployment.

Better deployment can improve cash generation.

More cash can fund the next bottleneck.

The system does not become bottleneck-free.

It becomes better at moving through bottlenecks.

That is a more realistic and more testable version of the original thesis.

### 3. Capital has option value beyond the NPV of one project

My earlier model treated capital mainly through return on invested capital.

That remains the final economic test, but I now think there is an additional layer in a highly uncertain technological regime.

Large financial capacity gives a company the ability to make more experiments, survive more mistakes and scale winners faster.

This only matters if the mistakes are survivable and the assets can be reused.

Amazon looks unusually strong on both dimensions.

A failed software experiment is cheap.

A failed compute assumption is more expensive, but the infrastructure may still be redeployed across AWS customers, inference, internal agents, retail, advertising or robotics.

A logistics or robotics investment can often produce learning even if the first implementation is imperfect.

This means I should not evaluate Amazon's capital base only as "how much money can management spend?"

I should also ask:

- How many technological paths can Amazon test?
- How many mistakes can it absorb?
- How quickly can it redirect the assets from a wrong path to a better one?
- How much learning survives the failure?

I now think this Deployment Optionality is economically meaningful in the current AI transition.

### 4. Friction data may be part of the learning mechanism

Another change is that I now think more explicitly about the value of operational friction.

Amazon does not only own clean demand data.

It observes where reality fails.

Orders arrive late.

Forecasts miss.

Robots fail to grip something.

Sellers generate unexpected returns.

Routes look efficient in software and fail in practice.

Ads receive clicks without conversions.

AWS workloads scale badly.

Inventory ends up in the wrong place.

Humans intervene when automated systems cannot handle the edge case.

Those failures are valuable because they show the difference between a model of the world and the real world.

Amazon can potentially run a loop of:

**action → friction → measurement → model/process change → redeployment → new outcome**

This is especially interesting because Amazon controls both digital and physical environments.

If parts of that learning can eventually be abstracted into AWS, agent systems, supply-chain tools or robotics products, Amazon can turn internal operational mistakes into external infrastructure.

I did not give enough weight to this possibility in the original decision.

### 5. Robotics may matter even if Amazon never dominates robotics hardware

I also think my earlier view of robotics was too narrow.

Amazon does not need to become the global winner in humanoid robots for robotics to matter economically.

Its more defensible advantage may be deployment.

Amazon has a huge real-world environment in which physical AI can be tested, measured and improved.

That creates a possible loop:

**warehouses → real robot interactions → failure data → better models → better robots → lower internal cost → larger deployment surface → more data**

AWS can sit under the same system through training, simulation, fleet intelligence and inference.

In the stronger case, Amazon could eventually provide parts of this stack to external customers.

The important asset would not be "the best robot."

It would be an unusually complete path from simulation and compute to real deployment and feedback.

That fits the larger thesis much better than simply forecasting the number of warehouse robots.

### 6. The sequence of events may be more favorable than I thought

The largest reason I was hesitant before was timing.

A prediction can be correct and still generate poor alpha if it takes ten years to become obvious while the market slowly prices it in.

I now think Amazon has a plausible intermediate path that can support the stock before the larger GDM thesis is proven.

The sequence can be:

**current CapEx → new AWS capacity → monetization of existing demand → higher operating cash generation → evidence on incremental ROIC → internal AI/robotics productivity → visible bottleneck-resolution advantages → broader revaluation**

The GDM thesis therefore does not need to carry the stock alone from day one.

The ordinary AWS and infrastructure business can buy the larger prediction time.

That changes the race condition.

The main risk is still that Amazon solves the physical bottlenecks successfully but at a cost that destroys the economics.

But the current evidence looks more like "capacity constrained in the presence of real demand" than "overbuilt infrastructure searching for customers."

That makes the sequence more investable.

## What has not changed

The most important bear case is still exactly the same:

**Amazon can be technologically right and economically wrong.**

The relevant test remains marginal return on capital.

USD 220 billion of CapEx is not bullish because the number is large.

Full data centers are not bullish merely because they are full.

A huge backlog is not the same thing as profit.

The economic chain still has to be:

**capital → infrastructure → usage → revenue → operating profit → cash flow → attractive incremental ROIC**

If the last step fails, the thesis fails as an investment even if AI demand explodes.

I am also still concerned about the quality of AI demand.

A large amount of contracted demand from a small number of highly capitalized AI labs is not economically identical to broad demand funded by thousands of end customers earning real returns from AI.

The healthier long-run transition is:

**capital-financed AI demand → broad self-funded productive AI demand.**

That is not proven yet.

The other major risks remain:

- Compute becomes increasingly commoditized.
- Hardware obsolescence and depreciation consume too much of the economics.
- Power, HBM, packaging, construction or regulation become expensive enough to transfer the rent away from Amazon.
- Amazon internalizes too many layers and replaces external dependence with internal complexity.
- Capital allocation becomes the real bottleneck.
- AI reduces competitors' coordination costs as much as or more than it reduces Amazon's.
- The stock can still underperform even if Amazon remains an excellent company because the starting valuation is already enormous.

None of these risks have disappeared.

That is why this is a USD 3,000 position and not a USD 10,000 position.

## Why USD 3,000

The position size is part of the thesis.

I do not think the evidence currently justifies a full high-conviction allocation.

A USD 3,000 position gives me exposure to the possibility that the intermediate mechanisms begin working earlier than expected without pretending that the central capital-return question is solved.

The logic is:

- USD 0 now feels too conservative because the thesis has become less binary and the price is lower than at my original rejection.
- USD 5,000 would imply more confidence in the next CapEx cycle than I currently have.
- USD 3,000 is enough to make the decision real while leaving room to add only if evidence improves.

I would rather scale after confirmation than size the full future thesis today.

## What would make me add

I would consider increasing the position if several of the following begin appearing together:

1. AWS operating income grows fast enough to make the current infrastructure wave look increasingly self-financing.
2. New capacity is monetized quickly without a major deterioration in AWS margins.
3. Free cash flow begins recovering even while Amazon continues investing heavily.
4. Demand becomes broader across industries and less dependent on a few capital-intensive AI labs.
5. Trainium creates measurable economic advantages rather than merely strategic independence.
6. Amazon shows structurally better output per employee or materially faster internal development cycles.
7. Robotics platforms become reusable across a wider set of tasks.
8. Amazon demonstrates that internal friction is being converted into repeatable software, agent or infrastructure products.
9. New businesses can use existing AWS, Marketplace, Advertising, Logistics or Robotics infrastructure with less additional organizational mass than in the past.
10. The market still values Amazon mostly as a collection of mature businesses while these reinforcement loops become increasingly visible.

The strongest signal would not be one spectacular quarter.

It would be acceleration in the system itself:

**Amazon becoming faster at becoming more productive.**

## What would make me reduce or exit

I would reduce the position if the race begins moving in the opposite direction:

- CapEx keeps rising while AWS growth and operating income slow materially.
- New capacity requires increasingly poor pricing or margins to remain utilized.
- Free cash flow does not recover after the current infrastructure comes online.
- Depreciation and replacement CapEx reveal that the apparent growth requires permanently extreme reinvestment.
- AI demand remains concentrated in companies dependent on continuous outside financing.
- Trainium or other internal infrastructure mainly shifts costs internally without creating a real economic advantage.
- Robotics remains highly task-specific with little evidence of reusable learning.
- Amazon's organizational complexity grows faster than AI reduces coordination costs.
- New businesses remain just as slow and expensive to launch as before.
- Regulation materially limits Amazon's ability to connect cloud, marketplace, advertising, logistics, devices or AI infrastructure.

A falling share price by itself is not a reason to exit.

A rising share price by itself is not proof that the thesis is working.

The decision should follow the causal chain.

## The updated thesis in one sentence

My original thesis was that Amazon could eventually become a Generalized Deployment Machine.

My updated thesis is more precise:

**Amazon may be building a system that can repeatedly identify the next bottleneck, deploy capital against it, learn from real-world friction, reuse infrastructure across digital and physical domains, and move economic resources toward new opportunities faster than competitors.**

If that happens, the important Amazon asset is no longer just AWS, Logistics, Advertising or Robotics separately.

It is the rate at which the whole system can convert capital and information into new productive capacity.

I still do not know whether that rate will remain high enough to justify hundreds of billions of dollars of annual investment.

That uncertainty is exactly why I am starting with USD 3,000 instead of pretending the full thesis is already proven.

## Decision

I am opening a **USD 3,000 virtual position in Amazon at approximately USD 260.97 per share on August 31, 2026**.

This does not invalidate the August 11 rejection.

The old decision remains part of the record because it reflected what I believed at the time.

The new decision records a genuine update:

- the price is lower,
- the ordinary AWS demand thesis remains strong,
- and my model of the larger Amazon opportunity has changed from a distant binary transformation to a sequence of smaller, testable and potentially reinforcing mechanisms.

The largest unresolved question remains marginal ROIC.

If Amazon proves that the current buildout creates high incremental economic returns while the bottleneck-resolution, friction-learning and deployment loops begin to appear, I will consider increasing the position.

For now, USD 3,000 is enough.
