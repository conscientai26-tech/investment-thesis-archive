# AI Capital Scarcity: What I Wrote on August 29, Before Jensen Huang Raised the Same Constraint

**Published:** October 23, 2026  
**Original thesis last edited in Notion:** August 29, 2026, 21:51 UTC  
**Relevant public statement:** Jensen Huang, CNBC interview, October 22, 2026  
**Original thesis:** [*Abundance Cycle — Kapital als Engpass* (Notion, German)](https://app.notion.com/p/3cb67b6787f88120a9dae87b1f3b3534) — see Section 24, **“Kapital kann selbst zum Engpass werden.”**

## Why I'm posting this

On October 22, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang told CNBC that demand for specialist AI cloud providers, or *neoclouds*, was extremely strong, but that there wasn't enough working capital to support the expansion they wanted. He described a shortage of capital relative to the number of projects and opportunities competing for it.

That caught my attention because I had written about exactly this type of bottleneck on **August 29, 2026**, almost eight weeks earlier.

I wasn't trying to predict the day Nvidia's CEO would discuss it. I was trying to understand what happens when an industry expands so aggressively that solving one shortage creates another. One of the explicit conclusions was that **capital itself could become the next bottleneck**.

I'm publishing this comparison because the original text is dated and remains accessible, and because I want to document where a framework is beginning to match observed developments—without rewriting it after the fact.

## What I wrote on August 29

The original note was called *Abundance Cycle — Kapital als Engpass*. In Section 24, I wrote:

> **“Kapital kann selbst zum Engpass werden”**
>
> “Wenn gleichzeitig jeder [...] Rechenzentren, Chipfabriken, Stromnetze, AI-Unternehmen, Cloud-Infrastruktur [...] bauen möchte, braucht das gigantisches Kapital.”
>
> “Irgendwann können Finanzierer sagen: Wir geben euch nicht mehr zu denselben Bedingungen Geld.”
>
> “Kapital wird teurer. Investitionen werden langsamer.”

In English, the argument was straightforward: if companies simultaneously want to build data centers, chip factories, power grids, AI businesses, and cloud infrastructure, the combined demand for funding can become enormous. At some point, financiers may refuse to offer money on the same terms. Capital becomes more expensive, and investment growth can slow.

I then described the feedback loop in the same section:

> “Compute war knapp. Kapital strömte hinein. Der Ausbau explodierte. Kapital wurde knapp. Der Ausbau verlangsamt sich. Der Cycle hat eine weitere Ebene erreicht.”

Translated:

**Compute was scarce → capital flowed in → expansion accelerated → capital became scarce → expansion slowed → the cycle moved to another layer.**

The broader theory wasn't that AI demand would disappear. It was almost the opposite: demand could remain strong while the *ability to finance the capacity needed to serve it* became the constraint.

That distinction matters. A market can have abundant customers and still be unable to expand at the speed those customers demand.

## What Huang said on October 22

At Nvidia's GTC conference in Berlin, Huang told CNBC that neocloud demand was **“off the charts.”** He pointed specifically to insufficient working capital to support growth and said demand for capital was greater than its availability. He also expected the problem to be solved.

Source: [CNBC — *Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang says neocloud demand is 'off the charts' but capital is scarce* (October 22, 2026)](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/22/nvidia-jensen-huang-neocloud-demand-capital.html).

This is the same kind of constraint I had outlined: the bottleneck can shift from the technical ability to produce compute toward the financial ability to deploy more of it.

The match is particularly interesting because it concerns a **second-order effect**. Building more capacity looks like the answer to compute scarcity. But building that capacity consumes another finite input: available financing on acceptable terms.

## The framework behind the prediction

My broader *Abundance Cycle* thesis treats scarcity as something that moves rather than simply vanishes.

1. New technology creates new demand.
2. A constrained input becomes valuable.
3. High returns attract capital and investment.
4. Expansion and innovation relieve that constraint.
5. The new level of activity places pressure on another limited resource.
6. A new bottleneck emerges.

In the original August note, I considered a possible progression through chips, electricity, grid connections, memory, capital, organization, and other constraints. **Capital was not added to that sequence in response to Huang's interview. It was already there.**

A related September 3 note expanded the same idea: even as AI makes some cognitive work much cheaper, access to capital and the physical resources needed to deploy AI may become *more* economically important, not less.

See: [*Abundance Cycle — Kapital als Engpass* (expanded September 3 note, German)](https://app.notion.com/p/3d067b6787f8817081b9e1f2c4a43feb).

## What this does and doesn't establish

**What the October 22 statement supports:** an important participant in the AI infrastructure ecosystem is explicitly identifying capital availability as a present constraint despite strong underlying demand. That is consistent with the mechanism I wrote down in August.

**What it does not yet prove:** that the whole AI buildout has already slowed because of capital scarcity; that every AI company faces the same problem; or that capital will remain scarce indefinitely. Huang himself expects funding solutions to emerge.

Neoclouds' working-capital and financing needs also shouldn't be confused with a universal shortage of money. The relative scarcity may be concentrated among capital-intensive operators competing for financing, especially when funding costs rise or lenders become more selective. Cash-rich hyperscalers may be in a different position.

The next useful observations are financing terms, access to debt and equity capital, canceled or deferred projects, utilization, infrastructure capex, and which businesses can continue expanding from internally generated cash flow.

## Why I think this is useful beyond one headline

I care less about predicting a particular news story than about building economic models that keep working when circumstances change.

If the bottleneck really is migrating toward capital, the consequences won't be equal across companies. Firms that need external financing for each new unit of capacity could become more vulnerable. Firms with substantial recurring cash flow, strong balance sheets, or the ability to finance infrastructure internally may gain flexibility and bargaining power.

But that is an investment hypothesis to test, not a conclusion that automatically follows from one interview.
